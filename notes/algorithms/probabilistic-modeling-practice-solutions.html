Probabilistic Modeling Practice: 20 Worked Problems

From probability fundamentals to latent-variable models, ranking, and sequential inference

Author

Published

1 A reusable modeling workflow

For each problem, move through the same sequence:

\boxed{ \text{random variables} \rightarrow \text{assumptions} \rightarrow \text{joint distribution} \rightarrow \text{desired marginal or conditional} }

Useful recurring tools:

Goal Default tool
Expected count Indicators + linearity of expectation
Probability of at least one event Complement or inclusion–exclusion
Infer a hidden cause Bayes’ rule
Predict a future observation Marginalize over posterior uncertainty
Estimate parameters Write and optimize the log-likelihood
Correct logged-feedback bias Condition on covariates or use propensities
Sequential latent inference Dynamic programming
TipInterview habit

Before calculating, state what is random, what is observed, and which conditional-independence assumptions justify the factorization. This often matters more than the final algebra.

2 Balls into buckets

2.1 Question

You throw n balls independently and uniformly into m buckets.

  1. What is the expected number of colliding pairs?
  2. What is the expected number of occupied buckets?
  3. What is the probability of at least one collision?
  4. Approximate the collision probability when n\ll m.

Concepts: indicators, linearity of expectation, birthday paradox, and Poisson approximation.

2.2 Solution

Throw n balls independently and uniformly into m buckets. Let B_i be the bucket selected by ball i.

2.3 Expected number of colliding pairs

For each pair i<j, define

I_{ij}=\mathbf 1\{B_i=B_j\}.

The collision-pair count is C=\sum_{i<j}I_{ij}. Since P(B_i=B_j)=1/m,

\boxed{E[C]=\binom n2\frac1m=\frac{n(n-1)}{2m}}.

The indicators need not be mutually independent; linearity of expectation does not require independence.

2.4 Expected number of occupied buckets

Let J_k indicate that bucket k is occupied. It is empty only if every ball avoids it:

P(J_k=0)=\left(1-\frac1m\right)^n.

Therefore

\boxed{E[O]=m\left[1-\left(1-\frac1m\right)^n\right]} \approx m(1-e^{-n/m}).

2.5 Probability of at least one collision

For n\le m,

P(C=0)=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{m}\right),

so

\boxed{P(C\ge1)=1-\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{m}\right)}.

If n>m, the probability is 1 by the pigeonhole principle.

When n\ll m, use \log(1-x)\approx-x:

P(C\ge1) \approx 1-\exp\left[-\frac{n(n-1)}{2m}\right].

For rare collisions, C is approximately Poisson with

\lambda=\frac{n(n-1)}{2m}.

If \lambda\ll1, then P(C\ge1)\approx\lambda.

3 Length-biased sampling

3.1 Question

Documents have random lengths L with distribution P(L=\ell). Select one token uniformly from all tokens in the corpus and inspect the document containing it.

  1. What is the distribution of that document’s length?
  2. Show that P(L=\ell\mid\text{random token})\propto \ell P(L=\ell).
  3. What is the expected length of the selected token’s document, and how does it compare with the document-level average E[L]?

Concepts: size-biased distributions, conditional probability, and the inspection paradox.

3.2 Solution

Let document length L have document-level distribution P(L=\ell)=p_\ell. Select one token uniformly from the entire corpus and inspect its document.

A document of length \ell contributes \ell possible sampled tokens, so it is \ell times as likely to be encountered as a unit-length document. Normalizing gives

\boxed{ P_{\text{token}}(L=\ell) =\frac{\ell p_\ell}{E[L]} }.

Its expected length is

E_{\text{token}}[L] =\sum_\ell \ell\frac{\ell p_\ell}{E[L]} =\boxed{\frac{E[L^2]}{E[L]}}.

Using E[L^2]=\operatorname{Var}(L)+E[L]^2,

\boxed{ E_{\text{token}}[L] =E[L]+\frac{\operatorname{Var}(L)}{E[L]} \ge E[L] }.

Equality holds only when all documents have the same length. This is the inspection paradox / size-bias effect.

4 Medical test and the base-rate effect

4.1 Question

A disease occurs in 1\% of people. A test satisfies

P(+\mid D)=0.95, \qquad P(+\mid\neg D)=0.05.

  1. Given a positive result, compute P(D\mid +).
  2. Recompute it if disease prevalence falls to 0.1\%.
  3. Explain why high sensitivity does not necessarily imply high precision.

Concepts: Bayes’ rule, the law of total probability, and base-rate effects.

4.2 Solution

Suppose

P(D)=0.01,\qquad P(+\mid D)=0.95,\qquad P(+\mid \neg D)=0.05.

Because D and \neg D partition the population,

P(+)=P(+\mid D)P(D)+P(+\mid\neg D)P(\neg D).

Bayes’ rule gives

P(D\mid+) =\frac{0.95(0.01)}{0.95(0.01)+0.05(0.99)} =\boxed{0.1610}.

With prevalence P(D)=0.001,

P(D\mid+) =\frac{0.95(0.001)}{0.95(0.001)+0.05(0.999)} =\boxed{0.0187}.

The sensitivity P(+\mid D) and the precision P(D\mid+) reverse the conditioning and can be very different.

NoteWhat must sum to one?

P(+\mid D)+P(-\mid D)=1 because the outcomes share the same conditioning event. In general, P(+\mid D)+P(+\mid\neg D) need not equal 1; it happens to do so for the supplied numbers.

5 Mixture of coins

5.1 Question

Choose one of two coins. Let Z\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.3), where Z=1 selects coin 1. Coin 1 has P(H)=0.8 and coin 2 has P(H)=0.4. You observe H,H,T,H.

  1. Compute P(Z=1\mid H,H,T,H).
  2. Compute the posterior predictive probability that the next toss is heads.

Concepts: latent variables, conditional independence, posterior inference, and posterior prediction.

5.2 Solution

Let Z=1 select coin 1 and Z=0 select coin 2:

P(Z=1)=0.3,\qquad P(H\mid Z=1)=0.8,\qquad P(H\mid Z=0)=0.4.

For X=(H,H,T,H), conditional independence of tosses gives

P(X\mid Z=1)=0.8^3(0.2)=0.1024,

P(X\mid Z=0)=0.4^3(0.6)=0.0384.

The unnormalized posterior weights are

w_1=0.3(0.1024)=0.03072, \qquad w_0=0.7(0.0384)=0.02688.

Thus

\boxed{P(Z=1\mid X)=\frac{w_1}{w_1+w_0}=0.5333}.

For the next toss, marginalize the uncertain coin identity:

\begin{aligned} P(H_{\text{next}}\mid X) &=\sum_zP(H_{\text{next}}\mid Z=z)P(Z=z\mid X)\\ &=0.8(0.5333)+0.4(0.4667)\\ &=\boxed{0.6133}. \end{aligned}

6 Beta–Bernoulli model

6.1 Question

Suppose

p\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta), \qquad X_i\mid p\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p).

After observing h heads and t tails:

  1. Derive the posterior distribution of p.
  2. Compute E[p\mid D].
  3. Compute the probability that the next toss is heads.

Concepts: conjugate priors, Bayesian updating, and posterior prediction.

6.2 Solution

Let

p\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta), \qquad X_i\mid p\overset{iid}{\sim}\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p).

After h heads and t tails, the likelihood is proportional to p^h(1-p)^t. Multiplying by the prior,

p(p\mid D) \propto p^{\alpha+h-1}(1-p)^{\beta+t-1}.

Therefore

\boxed{p\mid D\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha+h,\beta+t)}.

The posterior mean is

\boxed{E[p\mid D]=\frac{\alpha+h}{\alpha+\beta+h+t}}.

The next-toss probability is the posterior predictive:

\begin{aligned} P(X_{n+1}=1\mid D) &=\int P(X_{n+1}=1\mid p)p(p\mid D)\,dp\\ &=\int p\,p(p\mid D)\,dp\\ &=\boxed{\frac{\alpha+h}{\alpha+\beta+h+t}}. \end{aligned}

The prior acts like \alpha pseudo-heads and \beta pseudo-tails.

7 Dirichlet–categorical model

7.1 Question

A user’s clicks belong to K categories:

\theta\sim\operatorname{Dirichlet}(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_K), \qquad X_i\mid\theta\sim\operatorname{Categorical}(\theta).

After observing counts n_1,\ldots,n_K:

  1. Derive the posterior distribution of \theta.
  2. Derive the posterior predictive probability of category j.

Concepts: conjugacy, categorical models, and Bayesian smoothing.

7.2 Solution

Let

\theta\sim\operatorname{Dirichlet}(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_K), \qquad X_i\mid\theta\sim\operatorname{Categorical}(\theta).

Given counts n_1,\ldots,n_K, the likelihood is proportional to \prod_k\theta_k^{n_k}. Hence

\boxed{ \theta\mid D \sim\operatorname{Dirichlet}(\alpha_1+n_1,\ldots,\alpha_K+n_K) }.

Writing \alpha_0=\sum_k\alpha_k and n=\sum_kn_k, the predictive probability for category j is

\boxed{ P(X_{n+1}=j\mid D) =E[\theta_j\mid D] =\frac{\alpha_j+n_j}{\alpha_0+n} }.

This is Bayesian additive smoothing: unseen categories retain nonzero probability when \alpha_j>0.

8 Capture–recapture

8.1 Question

There are N fish in a lake. You catch n_1, mark them, and release them. Later, you catch n_2, of which k are marked.

  1. Write the likelihood of k given N.
  2. Estimate N.
  3. State the assumptions required for the estimate to be meaningful.

Concepts: the hypergeometric distribution, MLE, moment estimation, and latent population size.

8.2 Solution

There are N fish. Initially n_1 are caught, marked, and released. A second sample contains n_2 fish, k of which are marked.

Conditional on N, the second sample is hypergeometric:

\boxed{ P(k\mid N,n_1,n_2) =\frac{\binom{n_1}{k}\binom{N-n_1}{n_2-k}} {\binom{N}{n_2}} }.

The intuitive moment estimate equates the marked fraction in the population and second sample:

\frac{n_1}{N}\approx\frac{k}{n_2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\hat N\approx\frac{n_1n_2}{k}}.

The likelihood-based integer MLE is essentially the neighboring integer around n_1n_2/k (with boundary/tie details). A common small-sample bias correction is the Chapman estimator:

\boxed{ \hat N_{\text{Chapman}} =\frac{(n_1+1)(n_2+1)}{k+1}-1 }.

Required assumptions include population closure, equal catchability, reliable marks, and sufficient mixing between samples. When k=0, the naive estimate is infinite and the data provide only weak lower-bound information.

9 Poisson arrivals

9.1 Question

Requests arrive independently according to a Poisson process at rate \lambda=5 per minute. Find:

  1. the probability of exactly three requests in one minute;
  2. the probability of no requests for 30 seconds;
  3. the distribution of the time until the next request;
  4. the distribution and expected value of the waiting time until the tenth request.

Concepts: Poisson processes, exponential waiting times, and Gamma distributions.

9.2 Solution

Requests follow a Poisson process with rate \lambda=5 per minute.

9.3 Exactly three requests in one minute

\boxed{ P(N(1)=3)=e^{-5}\frac{5^3}{3!}\approx0.1404 }.

9.4 No requests for 30 seconds

Thirty seconds is 0.5 minute, so the expected count is 2.5:

\boxed{P(N(0.5)=0)=e^{-2.5}\approx0.0821}.

9.5 Time until the next request

\boxed{T_1\sim\operatorname{Exponential}(5)}, \qquad E[T_1]=\frac15\text{ minute}=12\text{ seconds}.

9.6 Time until the tenth request

It is the sum of ten independent exponential waiting times:

\boxed{T_{10}\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\text{shape}=10,\text{rate}=5)},

\boxed{E[T_{10}]=\frac{10}{5}=2\text{ minutes}}.

10 A/B testing

10.1 Question

Variant A receives 80 clicks from 1000 impressions, while variant B receives 100 clicks from 1000 impressions.

  1. Estimate the CTR difference p_B-p_A.
  2. Derive its estimated variance and a 95\% confidence interval.
  3. Test H_0:p_A=p_B against a two-sided alternative.
  4. With independent Beta priors, formulate and estimate P(p_B>p_A\mid D).

Concepts: binomial modeling, hypothesis testing, uncertainty, and frequentist versus Bayesian inference.

10.2 Solution

Variant A has 80/1000 clicks and B has 100/1000.

10.3 Point estimate and uncertainty

\hat p_A=0.08,\qquad \hat p_B=0.10,

\boxed{\widehat{p_B-p_A}=0.02}.

The unpooled estimated variance is

\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat p_B-\hat p_A) =\frac{\hat p_A(1-\hat p_A)}{n_A} +\frac{\hat p_B(1-\hat p_B)}{n_B}.

Numerically, SE\approx0.01279, giving the approximate 95\% confidence interval

0.02\pm1.96(0.01279) =\boxed{[-0.0051,\ 0.0451]}.

10.4 Two-sided test of equal CTR

Under H_0:p_A=p_B, the pooled estimate is

\hat p=\frac{80+100}{2000}=0.09.

Then

SE_0=\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)\left(\frac1{n_A}+\frac1{n_B}\right)} \approx0.01280,

z=\frac{0.10-0.08}{0.01280}\approx1.56.

The two-sided p-value is about 0.118, so this experiment does not reject equality at the 5\% level.

10.5 Bayesian comparison

With independent \operatorname{Beta}(1,1) priors,

p_A\mid D\sim\operatorname{Beta}(81,921),

p_B\mid D\sim\operatorname{Beta}(101,901).

The exact probability is

P(p_B>p_A\mid D) =\int_0^1 f_B(b)F_A(b)\,db,

which is approximately 0.94 here. This is not contradictory to the frequentist result: P(p_B>p_A\mid D) is not a p-value, and neither statement says the improvement is large enough to matter practically.

11 Simpson’s paradox

11.1 Question

Consider the following A/B results:

Segment A B
New users 10/100 90/1000
Existing users 810/900 80/100
  1. Which variant is better within each segment?
  2. Which variant is better overall?
  3. Do these data actually exhibit Simpson’s paradox? Explain how a reversal could arise.

Concepts: conditioning, confounding, aggregation, and weighted averages.

11.2 Solution

The supplied table is:

Segment A B
New users 10/100=10\% 90/1000=9\%
Existing users 810/900=90\% 80/100=80\%

Within both segments, A has the higher rate. Overall,

\hat p_A=\frac{820}{1000}=82\%,

\hat p_B=\frac{170}{1100}\approx15.45\%.

So A also wins overall.

WarningThe given numbers are not Simpson’s paradox

There is no reversal in this table. It demonstrates how dramatically different segment mixtures can affect aggregate rates, but both conditional and aggregate comparisons favor A.

In general, each aggregate is a weighted average:

P(Y=1\mid T=t) =\sum_sP(Y=1\mid T=t,S=s)P(S=s\mid T=t).

Simpson’s paradox occurs when A can have a higher rate within every segment, yet B has a higher aggregate rate because P(S\mid T=A) and P(S\mid T=B) differ sharply. Segment S is associated with both treatment assignment and outcome difficulty. The remedy is not blindly choosing segmented results; it is specifying the causal estimand and adjusting for genuine pre-treatment confounders.

12 Missing observations and selection bias

12.1 Question

Suppose user engagement Y is observed only if the user clicks:

C\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(e(X)),

and Y is visible only when C=1.

  1. Can E[Y\mid C=1] estimate overall engagement E[Y]?
  2. Under what assumptions is recovery possible?
  3. Derive an inverse-propensity-weighted estimator.
  4. What changes if selection depends on unobserved outcomes?

Concepts: selection bias, missingness, exchangeability, positivity, and inverse propensity weighting.

12.2 Solution

Suppose engagement Y is observed only when C=1, with

C\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(e(X)), \qquad e(X)=P(C=1\mid X).

In general,

E[Y\mid C=1]\ne E[Y],

because clicking selects a nonrepresentative population.

12.3 When is the clicked mean valid?

It is valid if observation is completely random with respect to outcome:

Y\perp C,

or under stronger designs that make the observed group representative. Usually this is implausible.

12.4 Adjustment using observed covariates

Assume:

  1. Conditional exchangeability / missing at random: Y\perp C\mid X.
  2. Positivity: e(X)>0 wherever the target population has mass.
  3. The propensity e(X) is known or consistently estimated.
  4. Relevant outcomes and covariates are measured consistently.

Then inverse-propensity weighting recovers the population mean:

\boxed{ E[Y] =E\left[\frac{CY}{e(X)}\right] }.

Indeed,

E\left[\frac{CY}{e(X)}\mid X,Y\right] =\frac{Y}{e(X)}E[C\mid X,Y] =Y.

If selection still depends on unobserved Y after conditioning on X—missing not at random—the overall mean is generally unidentified without extra assumptions, instruments, experiments, or sensitivity analysis.

13 Position bias in ranking

13.1 Question

A document has intrinsic binary relevance R, but clicks depend on both relevance and examination:

C=E\cdot R.

Suppose

P(E=1\mid\text{position}=k)=e_k.

  1. Given logged clicks, how can you estimate relevance?
  2. Why is inverse propensity weighting appropriate?
  3. What goes wrong if clicks are used directly as relevance labels?

Concepts: latent examination, position bias, propensity correction, and counterfactual learning.

13.2 Solution

Under the examination model,

C=E\cdot R,

where E indicates examination and R intrinsic relevance. At position k,

P(E=1\mid k)=e_k.

Assuming examination and relevance are conditionally independent given the displayed context,

P(C=1\mid k,x)=e_kP(R=1\mid x).

Thus an observed click at position k receives inverse-propensity weight 1/e_k. For a fixed item shown at positions k_i, an IPS-style relevance estimate is

\boxed{ \hat r =\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{C_i}{e_{k_i}} },

with clipping or self-normalization often used to control variance.

Training directly on clicks treats unexamined relevant results as negatives. It therefore learns the old ranker’s exposure policy and position effects along with relevance. Propensities may be estimated through randomized swaps/interleaving or an examination model; purely observational estimation requires additional identifiability assumptions.

14 Gaussian parameter estimation

14.1 Question

Suppose

X_i\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2).

  1. Derive the MLEs of \mu and \sigma^2.
  2. Why does the variance MLE use 1/n while the unbiased sample variance uses 1/(n-1)?

Concepts: likelihood, optimization, estimator bias, and degrees of freedom.

14.2 Solution

Let X_i\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2). The log-likelihood is

\ell(\mu,\sigma^2) =-\frac n2\log(2\pi)-\frac n2\log\sigma^2 -\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\mu)^2.

Differentiating with respect to \mu,

\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mu} =\frac1{\sigma^2}\sum_i(x_i-\mu)=0,

so

\boxed{\hat\mu_{\text{MLE}}=\bar x}.

Substituting \bar x and differentiating with respect to \sigma^2 gives

\boxed{ \hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}} =\frac1n\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2 }.

Why is this biased? Estimating \mu from the same data imposes

\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)=0,

leaving only n-1 independent residual degrees of freedom. In fact,

E\left[\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)^2\right]=(n-1)\sigma^2.

Therefore the unbiased estimator is

\boxed{s^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}.

MLE optimizes likelihood; it is not defined by unbiasedness.

15 Gaussian mixture model and EM

15.1 Question

Data come from

p(x)=\pi\mathcal N(x;\mu_1,\sigma_1^2) +(1-\pi)\mathcal N(x;\mu_2,\sigma_2^2),

where component assignment Z_i is unobserved.

  1. Derive P(Z_i=1\mid x_i).
  2. Derive the E-step and M-step updates for EM.
  3. State important limitations of EM for Gaussian mixtures.

Concepts: latent-variable inference, marginalization, responsibilities, and EM.

15.2 Solution

Let

p(x)=\pi\mathcal N(x;\mu_1,\sigma_1^2) +(1-\pi)\mathcal N(x;\mu_2,\sigma_2^2),

with latent assignment Z_i\in\{0,1\}.

15.3 Posterior responsibility

Bayes’ rule gives

\boxed{ \gamma_i :=P(Z_i=1\mid x_i) =\frac{\pi\mathcal N(x_i;\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)} {\pi\mathcal N(x_i;\mu_1,\sigma_1^2) +(1-\pi)\mathcal N(x_i;\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)} }.

15.4 EM algorithm

E-step: compute \gamma_i using the current parameters.

M-step: maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood:

N_1=\sum_i\gamma_i,\qquad N_2=\sum_i(1-\gamma_i),

\pi^{\text{new}}=\frac{N_1}{n},

\mu_1^{\text{new}}=\frac{\sum_i\gamma_ix_i}{N_1}, \qquad \mu_2^{\text{new}}=\frac{\sum_i(1-\gamma_i)x_i}{N_2},

(\sigma_1^2)^{\text{new}} =\frac{\sum_i\gamma_i(x_i-\mu_1^{\text{new}})^2}{N_1},

(\sigma_2^2)^{\text{new}} =\frac{\sum_i(1-\gamma_i)(x_i-\mu_2^{\text{new}})^2}{N_2}.

Repeat until the observed-data log-likelihood stops improving materially. EM monotonically increases—or leaves unchanged—the likelihood but may converge to a local optimum. Mixtures also have label-switching symmetry and can have singular likelihoods when a component variance collapses toward zero.

16 Hidden Markov model

16.1 Question

A user has an unobserved intent state

Z_t\in\{\text{shopping},\text{research},\text{navigation}\},

with transition model P(Z_t\mid Z_{t-1}) and observation model P(X_t\mid Z_t). Given X_{1:T}:

  1. How do you compute P(Z_t\mid X_{1:T})?
  2. How do you find the most likely complete state sequence?
  3. Why are these different inference problems?

Concepts: forward–backward, Viterbi, marginal MAP, joint MAP, and dynamic programming.

16.2 Solution

For latent intent states Z_{1:T} and observed queries X_{1:T},

p(z_{1:T},x_{1:T}) =p(z_1)p(x_1\mid z_1) \prod_{t=2}^Tp(z_t\mid z_{t-1})p(x_t\mid z_t).

16.3 Filtering and smoothing: forward–backward

Define the forward message

\alpha_t(j)=P(X_{1:t},Z_t=j).

Its recurrence is

\alpha_t(j) =P(X_t\mid Z_t=j)\sum_i\alpha_{t-1}(i)P(Z_t=j\mid Z_{t-1}=i).

Define the backward message

\beta_t(j)=P(X_{t+1:T}\mid Z_t=j),

with recurrence

\beta_t(i) =\sum_jP(Z_{t+1}=j\mid Z_t=i) P(X_{t+1}\mid Z_{t+1}=j)\beta_{t+1}(j).

Then the smoothed marginal is

\boxed{ P(Z_t=j\mid X_{1:T}) =\frac{\alpha_t(j)\beta_t(j)}{\sum_k\alpha_t(k)\beta_t(k)} }.

16.4 Most likely complete path: Viterbi

Define

\delta_t(j)=\max_{z_{1:t-1}}P(z_{1:t-1},Z_t=j,X_{1:t}).

Then

\delta_t(j) =P(X_t\mid Z_t=j) \max_i\left[\delta_{t-1}(i)P(Z_t=j\mid Z_{t-1}=i)\right].

Store each maximizing predecessor and backtrack at T.

Forward–backward computes a separate marginal MAP state at each time; Viterbi computes one joint MAP sequence. The individually most probable states need not form the most probable—or even structurally plausible—joint path.

17 Bayesian ranking and exploration

17.1 Question

For each item i,

p_i\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta),

and you observe c_i clicks from n_i impressions. Compare ranking items by:

  1. empirical CTR c_i/n_i;
  2. posterior mean E[p_i\mid D];
  3. a posterior sample \tilde p_i\sim P(p_i\mid D).

When should each score be used, and how do uncertainty and exposure bias affect the choice?

Concepts: Bayesian smoothing, uncertainty, exploration–exploitation, and Thompson sampling.

17.2 Solution

For item i,

p_i\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta),

with c_i clicks in n_i impressions. Then

p_i\mid D_i \sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha+c_i,\beta+n_i-c_i).

Ranking score Formula Behavior Best use
Empirical CTR c_i/n_i No shrinkage; unstable for small n_i Large, comparable sample sizes; descriptive ranking
Posterior mean (\alpha+c_i)/(\alpha+\beta+n_i) Shrinks uncertain items toward prior mean Stable exploitation and cold-start smoothing
Thompson sample \tilde p_i\sim p(p_i\mid D_i) Uncertain items sometimes rank high Online exploration–exploitation

Ranking by posterior mean maximizes immediate expected clicks under the model. Thompson sampling may sacrifice immediate reward to acquire information and can improve cumulative reward. If poor recommendations are costly, a conservative lower credible bound may be preferable.

NoteImportant logging issue

In a recommender, impressions are policy-dependent and affected by position. Beta–Bernoulli updating alone does not correct exposure bias; the observation model or experimental design must address it.

18 Coupon collector

18.1 Question

There are n equally likely categories. You repeatedly draw one category independently.

  1. What is the expected number of draws required to observe every category?
  2. Derive

E[T]=n\left(1+\frac12+\cdots+\frac1n\right).

  1. Give its large-n approximation.

Concepts: decomposition of expectations, geometric waiting times, and harmonic numbers.

18.2 Solution

There are n equally likely categories. After observing k distinct categories, the probability that the next draw is new is

\frac{n-k}{n}.

Let W_k be the additional draws needed to move from k to k+1 observed categories. Then

W_k\sim\operatorname{Geometric}\left(\frac{n-k}{n}\right),

so

E[W_k]=\frac{n}{n-k}.

Because T=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}W_k,

\begin{aligned} E[T] &=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{n}{n-k}\\ &=n\sum_{j=1}^n\frac1j\\ &=\boxed{nH_n}. \end{aligned}

Since H_n=\log n+\gamma+o(1),

\boxed{E[T]\approx n\log n+\gamma n}.

The last few unseen categories dominate the waiting time.

19 Maximum and minimum of uniform variables

19.1 Question

Let

X_1,\ldots,X_n\overset{iid}{\sim}\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1).

For M=\max_iX_i:

  1. derive its CDF and PDF;
  2. compute E[M].

Then derive the corresponding CDF, PDF, and expectation for m=\min_iX_i.

Concepts: order statistics, independence, and the CDF method.

19.2 Solution

Let X_1,\ldots,X_n\overset{iid}{\sim}\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1).

19.3 Maximum

For M=\max_iX_i and 0\le x\le1,

P(M\le x) =P(X_1\le x,\ldots,X_n\le x) =x^n.

Thus

\boxed{F_M(x)=x^n}, \qquad \boxed{f_M(x)=nx^{n-1}}.

The expectation is

\boxed{E[M]=\int_0^1x\,nx^{n-1}dx=\frac{n}{n+1}}.

19.4 Minimum

For m=\min_iX_i,

P(m>x)=P(X_1>x,\ldots,X_n>x)=(1-x)^n.

Therefore

\boxed{F_m(x)=1-(1-x)^n},

\boxed{f_m(x)=n(1-x)^{n-1}},

\boxed{E[m]=\frac1{n+1}}.

The key trick is to derive the CDF from an event involving all independent variables.

20 Random walk and gambler’s ruin

20.1 Question

A particle begins at X_0 and moves independently at each step:

X_{t+1}= \begin{cases} X_t+1, & \text{with probability }p,\\ X_t-1, & \text{with probability }1-p. \end{cases}

  1. Compute E[X_t] and \operatorname{Var}(X_t).
  2. Starting at i\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}, compute the probability of hitting N before 0.
  3. Compute the expected time to hit either boundary.

Concepts: random walks, Markov chains, first-step recurrences, and gambler’s ruin.

20.2 Solution

Let increments Y_s\in\{+1,-1\} satisfy

P(Y_s=1)=p,\qquad P(Y_s=-1)=q=1-p,

and X_t=X_0+\sum_{s=1}^tY_s.

20.3 Mean and variance

Since E[Y_s]=p-q=2p-1 and \operatorname{Var}(Y_s)=4pq,

\boxed{E[X_t]=X_0+t(2p-1)},

\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=4tpq}.

20.4 Probability of hitting N before 0

Let

h_i=P_i(\tau_N<\tau_0).

First-step analysis gives

h_i=ph_{i+1}+qh_{i-1}, \qquad h_0=0,\quad h_N=1.

Solving,

\boxed{ h_i= \begin{cases} \dfrac{1-(q/p)^i}{1-(q/p)^N}, & p\ne q,\\[8pt] \dfrac{i}{N}, & p=q=1/2. \end{cases} }

20.5 Expected time to hit either boundary

Let e_i=E_i[\tau_{\{0,N\}}]. Then

e_i=1+pe_{i+1}+qe_{i-1}, \qquad e_0=e_N=0.

The solution is

\boxed{ e_i= \begin{cases} \dfrac{Nh_i-i}{p-q}, & p\ne q,\\[8pt] i(N-i), & p=q=1/2. \end{cases} }

For the unbiased walk, the expected position does not drift, yet absorption still occurs and can take O(N^2) time from the middle.

21 Probabilistic graphical-model reasoning

21.1 Question

Consider the common-cause graph

X\leftarrow Z\rightarrow Y.

  1. Are X and Y independent marginally?
  2. Are they independent conditioned on Z?

Now consider the collider

X\rightarrow Z\leftarrow Y.

  1. Are X and Y independent marginally?
  2. What happens after conditioning on Z?

Concepts: factorization, conditional independence, d-separation, common causes, colliders, and explaining away.

21.2 Solution

21.3 Common cause: X\leftarrow Z\rightarrow Y

The joint factorizes as

p(x,y,z)=p(z)p(x\mid z)p(y\mid z).

Therefore

\boxed{X\perp Y\mid Z}.

Marginally,

p(x,y)=\sum_zp(z)p(x\mid z)p(y\mid z),

which generally does not equal p(x)p(y). Thus X and Y are usually dependent because observing one provides information about their common cause.

21.4 Collider: X\rightarrow Z\leftarrow Y

The joint factorizes as

p(x,y,z)=p(x)p(y)p(z\mid x,y).

Assuming no other path connects them,

\boxed{X\perp Y}

marginally. But conditioning on Z gives

p(x,y\mid z)\propto p(x)p(y)p(z\mid x,y),

which generally does not factorize. Therefore

\boxed{X\not\!\perp Y\mid Z}.

This is collider bias or explaining away. If admission depends on either high grades or athletic ability, then among admitted students, learning that one student has weaker grades raises the probability that they have strong athletic ability—even if the traits were independent in the applicant population.

22 Final synthesis

These problems fall into a small number of reusable modeling patterns:

Pattern Problems Core move
Indicator decomposition Buckets, coupon collector Convert a global count into local random variables
Reweighting by observation process Length bias, missingness, position bias Model why an observation entered the data
Bayes and marginalization Medical test, coin mixture, conjugate models Posterior \propto likelihood \times prior
Likelihood-based estimation Capture–recapture, Gaussian MLE, GMM Write the data-generating distribution first
Aggregation and confounding A/B testing, Simpson’s paradox Separate estimand, uncertainty, and assignment mechanism
Sequential dynamic programming HMM, random walk Condition on the next/previous state
Uncertainty-aware decisions Bayesian ranking Distinguish inference from action selection
ImportantSenior MLE / Research Engineer takeaway

The unifying question is not “which formula applies?” It is “what observation process generated this dataset?” Most production failures—selection bias, position bias, confounding, feedback loops, and overconfident ranking—come from modeling outcomes while ignoring that process.
