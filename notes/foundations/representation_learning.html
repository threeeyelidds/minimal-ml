Representation Learning from First Principles

The Problem

Raw observations are rarely presented in the coordinates that make a useful decision easy. An image is an array of pixels, a sentence is a sequence of token IDs, and a user history is a sequence of sparse item IDs. A representation learner constructs

\[ z=f_\theta(x), \]

so that another model can solve useful tasks from \(z\) more easily than from the raw input \(x\).

A representation is therefore not merely a smaller input. It is a choice of what information to preserve, what variation to ignore, and which relationships should become simple in the new space.

ImportantThere is no universally best representation

Without a task, environment, or structural assumption, every invertible transformation of \(x\) preserves the same information. Representation learning becomes meaningful only after specifying which distinctions matter and which transformations should not matter.

For a family of downstream tasks \(\mathcal T\), one operational objective is

\[ \min_f\; \mathbb E_{T\sim\mathcal T} \left[ \min_{h\in\mathcal H_{ ext{simple}}} R_T(h\circ f) \right]. \]

The readout class \(\mathcal H_{ ext{simple}}\) is deliberately restricted—for example, to linear models or shallow networks. If an arbitrarily powerful readout is allowed, it can relearn the structure that the representation failed to expose.

What should a useful representation provide?

Property Desired behavior Why it helps Failure mode
Sufficiency retain information needed for the task accurate downstream prediction useful signal is discarded
Invariance ignore specified nuisance transformations sample efficiency and robustness a meaningful distinction is erased
Equivariance transform predictably when the input transforms preserves pose, location, time, or order spatial structure disappears
Smoothness nearby or related inputs have nearby representations interpolation and simple decision boundaries shortcuts define the neighborhood
Non-collapse use multiple examples and dimensions preserves diversity every input or dimension becomes identical
Compression discard irrelevant detail regularization and efficiency excessive compression loses task signal
Accessibility make useful factors easy for a simple readout to recover few labels and cheap adaptation information exists but is entangled

These properties compete. Classification benefits from invariance to object position, while detection needs position to remain recoverable. A global image embedding may transfer well to classification but poorly to dense prediction.

A Generative View: Signal and Nuisance

Suppose an observation is generated from semantic factors \(s\) and nuisance factors \(n\):

\[ x=g(s,n). \]

If downstream targets depend on \(s\) but not \(n\), we want \(z\) to retain \(s\) and discard \(n\). This simple statement exposes the central difficulty: without labels or prior knowledge, the learner does not know which factor is semantic.

We inject that knowledge through a learning signal:

  • labels declare which examples should share a decision
  • augmentations declare which transformations preserve identity
  • temporal or spatial context declares what should be predictable
  • reconstruction declares which parts of the input must be retained
  • paired modalities declare that two observations describe shared content
  • an architecture encodes locality, order, symmetry, or capacity constraints

Representation learning is therefore the design of an information bottleneck and the supervision that shapes it—not just the choice of encoder.

Three First-Principles Objectives

Most representation-learning methods combine three pressures.

Preserve task-relevant information

For a known target \(Y\), sufficiency asks that

\[ P(Y\mid Z)=P(Y\mid X), \]

or equivalently, in information-theoretic language,

\[ I(Z;Y)=I(X;Y). \]

Supervised cross-entropy directly encourages this property for one label space. It may discard information useful to other tasks.

Remove nuisance information

If \(t\) is a transformation known to preserve semantics, invariance asks for

\[ f(t(x))\approx f(x). \]

For transformations whose effect matters, equivariance is more appropriate:

\[ f(t(x))\approx \rho(t)f(x), \]

where \(\rho(t)\) is the corresponding transformation in representation space. The distinction is crucial: color jitter might be a safe invariance for shape recognition, while spatial translation should remain equivariant for localization.

Keep the representation non-degenerate

The invariance objective alone has the trivial optimum \(f(x)=c\). A useful objective therefore needs a counterforce that preserves diversity, such as:

  • reconstructing or predicting held-out information
  • separating unrelated examples with negatives
  • maintaining variance across examples
  • decorrelating dimensions
  • using an asymmetric teacher–student optimization path

Modern self-supervised methods differ substantially in how they supply this counterforce.

Geometry First: Graph and Spectral Representations

The supplied Representation Learning course notes begin from a useful non-neural perspective: define which observations are related, then find coordinates that vary slowly across those relationships.

Given samples \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\), construct a symmetric similarity matrix \(W\). For example,

\[ W_{ij} = \begin{cases} \exp\left(-\dfrac{\lVert x_i-x_j\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), & i\ne j,\\ 0, & i=j. \end{cases} \]

Let \(D_{ii}=\sum_j W_{ij}\) and define the unnormalized graph Laplacian \(L=D-W\). For any scalar feature vector \(v\in\mathbb R^n\),

\[ v^TLv = \frac12 \sum_{i,j}W_{ij}(v_i-v_j)^2. \]

Minimizing this energy makes strongly connected examples receive similar coordinates. Constant vectors minimize it trivially, so Laplacian eigenmaps add normalization and orthogonality constraints. One common formulation is

\[ \min_Z \operatorname{tr}(Z^TLZ) \quad\text{subject to}\quad Z^TDZ=I, \qquad Z^TD\mathbf 1=0. \]

The solution is given by the nontrivial generalized eigenvectors

\[ Lv=\lambda Dv. \]

This is representation learning by an explicitly chosen geometry. Spectral clustering uses the embedding to expose clusters; Laplacian eigenmaps interpret the same low-frequency eigenvectors as smooth coordinates on an underlying manifold.

What the derivation teaches

  • A distance metric or similarity graph is already an inductive bias.
  • The bandwidth and neighborhood rule decide which local relationships exist.
  • Eigenvectors are identifiable only up to sign, and a repeated eigenspace is identifiable only up to a rotation.
  • Local geometry can reveal structure that Euclidean coordinates obscure, but constructing an \(n\times n\) graph does not scale naively.

Reconstruction: PCA to Autoencoders

Another principle is to encode \(x\) so that it can be reconstructed. Let centered \(x\in\mathbb R^d\) be encoded into \(k<d\) dimensions by

\[ z=W^Tx, \qquad W^TW=I, \]

and reconstructed as \(\hat x=Wz\). PCA solves

\[ \min_W \mathbb E\lVert x-WW^Tx\rVert_2^2. \]

Expanding the square shows that this is equivalent to maximizing retained variance:

\[ \max_{W^TW=I} \operatorname{tr}(W^T\Sigma W). \]

Thus the columns of \(W\) are the top eigenvectors of the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\). The minimum reconstruction error is the sum of discarded eigenvalues:

\[ R(k)=\sum_{j=k+1}^{d}\lambda_j. \]

A linear autoencoder with squared-error loss learns the same principal subspace as PCA, although its coordinates can differ by an invertible change of basis.

import torch


def fit_pca(x, num_components):
    """Return the mean, principal directions, and low-dimensional codes."""
    mean = x.mean(dim=0, keepdim=True)
    centered = x - mean
    _, _, right_vectors = torch.linalg.svd(centered, full_matrices=False)
    directions = right_vectors[:num_components].T
    codes = centered @ directions
    return mean, directions, codes


torch.manual_seed(0)
latent = torch.randn(512, 2)
mixing = torch.randn(2, 8)
samples = latent @ mixing + 0.02 * torch.randn(512, 8)

mean, directions, codes = fit_pca(samples, num_components=2)
reconstruction = codes @ directions.T + mean
relative_error = (samples - reconstruction).norm() / samples.norm()
assert relative_error < 0.03

Nonlinear autoencoders

A nonlinear encoder \(f_\theta\) and decoder \(g_\phi\) minimize

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{AE}} = \mathbb E_x \left[ d\bigl(x,g_\phi(f_\theta(x))\bigr) \right]. \]

The reconstruction metric \(d\) determines what “information” means. Pixel MSE rewards exact texture and color; a likelihood models a full observation distribution; a perceptual or latent target emphasizes different structure.

Flexible autoencoders reveal two limitations:

  1. With enough capacity, they can learn an identity map without discovering a useful abstraction.
  2. For any invertible \(h\), the pair \(h\circ f\) and \(g\circ h^{-1}\) reconstructs equally well. The latent coordinates are therefore not identifiable from reconstruction alone.

Bottlenecks, noise, sparsity, masking, quantization, and probabilistic priors change what information is economical to retain.

Information Is Not the Same as Usefulness

One might try to maximize \(I(X;Z)\). But a deterministic invertible encoder can retain all information while producing coordinates that are awkward for every simple downstream model. Conversely, an invariant representation intentionally throws information away.

The information-bottleneck ideal makes the trade-off explicit:

\[ \max_f I(Z;Y)-\beta I(Z;X). \]

The first term seeks target sufficiency; the second penalizes unnecessary input information. This is a conceptual guide rather than a complete recipe: mutual information is hard to estimate in high dimensions, and the correct target \(Y\) may be unavailable during pretraining.

The more operational question is:

Which information becomes recoverable by a simple learner, with few labels, under the distribution shifts we care about?

The same abstraction across fields

Tokenization chooses the discrete or continuous units an encoder consumes; representation learning chooses the geometry and information carried by their contextual states. The same principles then recur across domains:

Domain Observation Useful equivalence or prediction Typical representation
Language token sequence masked/next token, paraphrase, document–query relevance contextual token states or pooled text embedding
Vision patches or pixels augmented views, masked regions, image–caption pairing global image vector plus local patch features
Audio/video waveform, frames, or codes masked spans, temporal continuation, synchronized modalities time-indexed features and pooled clip embedding
Recommendation sparse user/item IDs and event sequence click/purchase pairs, next item, co-consumption user and item vectors in a retrieval space
Graphs nodes, edges, and attributes neighborhoods, masked nodes/edges, graph identity node, edge, or whole-graph embedding
Control state/action trajectory next state, reward, reachability, behavioral similarity compact decision-relevant state

The equivalence relation must match the product. In a two-tower recommender, dot-product geometry is chosen because approximate nearest-neighbor retrieval must cheaply recover relevant items. In control, two observations that look different may deserve the same state representation if they imply the same rewards and transition behavior.

Supervised representations

If \(z=f_\theta(x)\) feeds a classifier \(p_\phi(y\mid z)\), minimizing

\[ -\mathbb E\log p_\phi(y\mid f_\theta(x)) \]

encourages \(z\) to make the training labels predictable. The labels define the equivalence classes. This is powerful but narrow: two dog breeds are encouraged to separate even when another task only needs “dog,” and variations within one class can disappear even when they matter elsewhere.

Metric-learning losses instead directly specify local geometry. A triplet loss, for example, requires an anchor \(a\) to be closer to a positive \(p\) than a negative \(n\) by a margin \(m\):

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{triplet}} = \left[ d(z_a,z_p)-d(z_a,z_n)+m \right]_+. \]

The sampling policy often matters as much as the formula because easy negatives provide little signal and false negatives teach the wrong geometry.

Contrastive Learning

Let \(z_i\) and \(z_i^+\) be embeddings of two views declared equivalent, and let the other examples in a batch act as negatives. An InfoNCE loss is

\[ \mathcal L_i = -\log \frac{\exp(\operatorname{sim}(z_i,z_i^+)/\tau)} {\sum_j\exp(\operatorname{sim}(z_i,z_j)/\tau)}. \]

It combines:

  • alignment: positive views should be close
  • uniformity/separation: the population should not collapse to one point

Under its sampling assumptions, InfoNCE also gives the mutual-information lower bound \(I\ge \log N-\mathcal L_{\text{NCE}}\), where \(N\) is the number of candidates. This interpretation is useful but incomplete: downstream quality depends on the view construction and representation geometry, not just on making the bound large.

import torch.nn.functional as F


def symmetric_info_nce(view_a, view_b, temperature=0.1):
    """Minimal in-batch contrastive loss for paired representations."""
    view_a = F.normalize(view_a, dim=-1)
    view_b = F.normalize(view_b, dim=-1)
    logits = view_a @ view_b.T / temperature
    labels = torch.arange(logits.shape[0], device=logits.device)
    return 0.5 * (
        F.cross_entropy(logits, labels)
        + F.cross_entropy(logits.T, labels)
    )


base = F.normalize(torch.randn(16, 32), dim=-1)
matching = base + 0.01 * torch.randn_like(base)
shuffled = matching[torch.randperm(matching.shape[0])]
assert symmetric_info_nce(base, matching) < symmetric_info_nce(base, shuffled)

Augmentations define semantics

SimCLR made this especially visible: augmentation composition is not incidental regularization; it defines the pretext task. If cropping removes the object or if color is label-relevant, forcing invariance creates a false positive. If two different instances share semantics, treating all other batch items as negatives creates false negatives.

The correct positive-pair policy is an assumption about the world.

Learning Without Negatives

If two views are merely pulled together, the constant representation is a global optimum. Negative-free methods prevent collapse in different ways:

  • BYOL: an online network predicts a stop-gradient target generated by a slowly moving teacher; predictor and update asymmetry are essential parts of the system.
  • Barlow Twins: makes the cross-correlation of two views approach the identity, combining invariance with redundancy reduction.
  • VICReg: explicitly combines view invariance, per-dimension variance, and covariance regularization.
  • DINO-style self-distillation: centers and sharpens teacher distributions while using a momentum teacher and multi-crop views.

For a batch representation \(Z\in\mathbb R^{B\times d}\), VICReg can be summarized as

\[ \mathcal L = \lambda\mathcal L_{\text{inv}} +\mu\mathcal L_{\text{var}} +\nu\mathcal L_{\text{cov}}. \]

The terms respectively align paired views, keep each dimension’s batch standard deviation above a threshold, and penalize off-diagonal covariance.

def off_diagonal(matrix):
    size = matrix.shape[0]
    return matrix.flatten()[:-1].view(size - 1, size + 1)[:, 1:].flatten()


def vicreg_loss(view_a, view_b, variance_target=1.0, eps=1e-4):
    """Compact VICReg objective with equal weights for exposition."""
    invariance = F.mse_loss(view_a, view_b)

    std_a = torch.sqrt(view_a.var(dim=0) + eps)
    std_b = torch.sqrt(view_b.var(dim=0) + eps)
    variance = F.relu(variance_target - std_a).mean()
    variance += F.relu(variance_target - std_b).mean()

    centered_a = view_a - view_a.mean(dim=0)
    centered_b = view_b - view_b.mean(dim=0)
    covariance_a = centered_a.T @ centered_a / (view_a.shape[0] - 1)
    covariance_b = centered_b.T @ centered_b / (view_b.shape[0] - 1)
    covariance = off_diagonal(covariance_a).square().mean()
    covariance += off_diagonal(covariance_b).square().mean()

    return invariance + variance + covariance


noncollapsed = torch.randn(64, 16)
constant = torch.zeros_like(noncollapsed)
assert vicreg_loss(constant, constant) > vicreg_loss(
    noncollapsed, noncollapsed + 0.01 * torch.randn_like(noncollapsed)
)
NoteCollapse has more than one form

Complete collapse maps every example to the same vector. Dimensional collapse uses only a low-rank subspace. A representation can also avoid both while learning a shortcut that transfers poorly. Batch variance alone therefore does not prove semantic quality.

Masked and Predictive Representations

Reconstruction becomes a stronger abstraction pressure when the model sees only partial context.

Masked observation prediction

BERT predicts masked tokens from bidirectional context. MAE masks a high fraction of image patches, encodes only visible patches, and uses a lightweight decoder to reconstruct missing pixels. In general,

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{mask}} = \sum_{i\in\mathcal M} d\left(y_i, g_\phi(f_\theta(x_{\setminus\mathcal M}),i) \right). \]

The target \(y_i\) matters. Pixels emphasize appearance; discrete tokens depend on the tokenizer; learned latent targets can emphasize more predictable semantic structure. Mask ratio and mask shape determine how much context must be integrated rather than copied.

Predict in representation space

Joint-embedding predictive architectures avoid reconstructing every observation detail. I-JEPA encodes a context region and predicts the teacher representation of masked target blocks:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{JEPA}} = \sum_{j} d\left( p_\psi(f_\theta(x_{\text{context}}),m_j), \operatorname{sg}\left[f_{\bar\theta}(x_{\text{target},j})\right] \right). \]

Predicting in latent space says that unpredictable pixel-level detail need not be modeled. The masking strategy still defines the abstraction: large target blocks and spatially distributed context encourage semantic, nonlocal features.

Autoregressive prediction

Next-token prediction is also representation learning. Hidden states must encode information that helps predict the continuation:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{NTP}} = -\sum_t\log p(x_t\mid x_{<t}). \]

Its great advantage is a universal training signal over raw sequences. Its representation is shaped by tokenization, causal direction, and the demands of generation; the last layer is not automatically the best frozen embedding for every downstream task.

Cross-Modal Alignment

Paired modalities provide supervision about shared latent causes. CLIP learns image and text encoders by identifying matching pairs in a batch. This replaces a fixed class vocabulary with concepts expressible through language and enables zero-shot classification and retrieval.

SigLIP replaces batch-normalized softmax competition with an independent binary classification loss for each image–text pair:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{sigmoid}} = \sum_{i,j} \log\left( 1+\exp[-y_{ij}(t\,z_i^Tv_j+b)] \right), \]

where \(y_{ij}=1\) for a matched pair and \(-1\) otherwise. This removes the need for a global softmax over all pairs.

Cross-modal alignment preserves the intersection of what the modalities make predictable. Caption supervision gives strong semantics, but captions may omit precise geometry, texture, or unmentioned objects. Recent systems therefore mix objectives rather than expect one loss to provide every property.

The Modern View: Objective Mixtures and Data

The field has moved from searching for one universal self-supervised loss toward assembling complementary learning signals.

Method family Core pressure Anti-collapse / information mechanism Characteristic bias
SimCLR / CPC distinguish a positive from negatives negative examples augmentation- or context-defined invariance
BYOL / DINO match a teacher across views optimization asymmetry and teacher dynamics global semantic similarity
VICReg / Barlow Twins align views explicit variance and decorrelation nonredundant batch features
MAE reconstruct masked observations reconstruction target retains information useful for filling gaps
I-JEPA predict masked latent targets teacher targets and context prediction predictable semantic structure
CLIP / SigLIP align paired modalities cross-example or pairwise discrimination shared language–vision semantics
DINOv2 scaled self-distillation and masked objectives teacher–student recipe plus curated data general global and dense visual features
SigLIP 2 image–text alignment plus captioning, self-distillation, and masked prediction complementary objectives and online curation multilingual, semantic, and localized features

Two current lessons are easy to miss:

  1. The dataset defines the representation. DINOv2 emphasizes a diverse, curated image pipeline; modern contrastive systems increasingly treat filtering, deduplication, and mixture design as part of the learning method.
  2. Global and local features need different pressure. Classification, retrieval, localization, and dense prediction do not reward identical invariances. DINOv2 and SigLIP 2 explicitly target broader transfer than a single global benchmark.

Representation capacity is also a systems choice

A fixed embedding dimension forces every downstream application to pay the same storage, communication, and retrieval cost. Matryoshka Representation Learning trains multiple nested prefixes of one vector to be useful:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{MRL}} = \sum_{m\in\mathcal M} \alpha_m \mathcal L\bigl(z_{1:m}\bigr). \]

The first dimensions learn a coarse representation, while longer prefixes add detail. One model can therefore trade accuracy for latency or index size at deployment without training an independent encoder for every dimension. This is a modern example of designing a representation jointly with its downstream systems constraints.

Identifiability and What the Coordinates Mean

If an objective uses only a decoder \(g(f(x))\), an invertible transformation of the latent may leave the objective unchanged. Contrastive objectives based on dot products have their own rotational symmetries. Consequently:

  • a latent dimension is not automatically a human concept
  • two independently trained models can encode similar structure in different bases
  • probing a coordinate does not establish a causal or disentangled factor
  • high downstream accuracy does not prove that nuisance information is absent

Disentanglement cannot generally be recovered from observational data without inductive biases or supervision. Interpretability claims must state which equivalences the training objective leaves unresolved.

How to Evaluate a Representation

No single score establishes representation quality.

Evaluation What is held fixed? What it tests Caveat
Linear probe encoder linear accessibility sensitive to optimization and feature normalization
\(k\)-NN encoder and nearly all learning capacity local geometry distance metric and \(k\) matter
Few-shot probe encoder label efficiency high variance across task samples
Full fine-tuning little initialization and adaptability can hide weak frozen features
Retrieval encoder cross-example or cross-modal geometry benchmark negatives may be unrepresentative
Dense prediction often encoder initially local spatial information global embeddings may look deceptively strong elsewhere
Shift/robustness tests training distribution stability of claimed invariances shifts must reflect the deployment setting

Also inspect the representation itself:

  • per-dimension variance and covariance
  • singular-value spectrum and effective rank
  • positive-pair alignment and population spread
  • nearest neighbors and failure cases
  • sensitivity to transformations that should be invariant or equivariant
  • leakage of nuisance or protected attributes

The best evaluation is a matrix of tasks, data regimes, and adaptation budgets that matches the intended use.

A Design Checklist

Before choosing an objective, answer:

  1. What future decisions should \(z\) support? This defines sufficiency.
  2. Which transformations preserve meaning? These define invariances.
  3. Which transformations must remain observable? These call for equivariance or local features.
  4. Where do positive relations come from? Labels, views, context, time, or paired modalities each encode different assumptions.
  5. What prevents collapse or trivial copying? Negatives, bottlenecks, prediction, variance, or teacher asymmetry must do real work.
  6. Which target is predicted? Pixels, tokens, latent codes, and future states preserve different information.
  7. How will transfer be measured? Use the same adaptation and distribution conditions expected downstream.

  • Derive the graph-Laplacian smoothness identity \(v^TLv\).
  • How do spectral embeddings turn a similarity assumption into coordinates?
  • Show why linear squared-error autoencoders recover the PCA subspace.
  • Why are nonlinear autoencoder latents non-identifiable?
  • Contrast invariance and equivariance with an example where invariance is harmful.
  • Why does positive-pair alignment alone collapse?
