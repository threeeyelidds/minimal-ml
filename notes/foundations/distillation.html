Distillation: Learning from a Teacher

Distillation Is a Source of Supervision

Let \(q\) denote a teacher and \(p_\theta\) a student. Distillation transfers information exposed by the teacher that is not contained in a single hard label:

  • uncertainty across classes or tokens
  • similarities between alternatives
  • intermediate representations
  • generated solutions or trajectories
  • behavior on states selected by either teacher or student

The defining property is not one particular formula. It is that the training target comes from another model. A distilled student may be discriminative, generative, representational, or policy-like.

Three choices define a distillation objective:

  1. What is matched? Outputs, logits, features, relations, sequences, or behavior.
  2. Where is it matched? Data inputs, teacher-generated inputs, or student-generated/on-policy states.
  3. How is it matched? Forward KL, reverse KL, MSE, cosine distance, sequence NLL, or an RL-style objective.

Output Distillation: Soft Targets

For class or token logits \(z^T\) and \(z^S\), temperature \(\tau\) defines

\[ q_\tau(i\mid x) = \operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{z_i^T}{\tau}\right), \qquad p_{\theta,\tau}(i\mid x) = \operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{z_i^S}{\tau}\right). \]

Classical output distillation minimizes forward KL:

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{KD}} = \tau^2 \operatorname{KL} \left(q_\tau(\cdot\mid x)\|p_{\theta,\tau}(\cdot\mid x)\right). \]

The teacher supplies the expectation, so missing a teacher-supported class is costly. The entropy of \(q_\tau\) is constant with respect to the student; therefore this is equivalent to soft-label cross-entropy up to a constant.

Larger \(\tau\) reveals relative preferences among non-maximum classes. Dividing logits by \(\tau\) also shrinks their gradients, so the conventional \(\tau^2\) factor approximately preserves gradient scale when comparing temperatures.

A common supervised mixture is

\[ \mathcal L = (1-\lambda)\operatorname{CE}(y,p_{\theta,1}) + \lambda\tau^2 \operatorname{KL}(q_\tau\|p_{\theta,\tau}). \]

The hard label anchors the student to observed outcomes; the soft target transfers the teacher’s richer geometry. A teacher can also be wrong, biased, or miscalibrated, so the mixture is not automatically better than either term.

Minimal implementation

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F


def forward_kl_distillation(student_logits, teacher_logits, temperature=2.0):
    """Teacher-to-student KL, averaged over the batch."""
    log_p_student = F.log_softmax(student_logits / temperature, dim=-1)
    p_teacher = F.softmax(teacher_logits / temperature, dim=-1)
    return (
        F.kl_div(
            log_p_student,
            p_teacher,
            reduction="batchmean",
        )
        * temperature**2
    )


torch.manual_seed(0)
teacher_logits = torch.randn(8, 10)
close_student = teacher_logits + 0.01 * torch.randn_like(teacher_logits)
far_student = torch.randn_like(teacher_logits)
assert forward_kl_distillation(
    close_student, teacher_logits
) < forward_kl_distillation(far_student, teacher_logits)

Detach teacher outputs unless the teacher is intentionally co-trained. Otherwise the objective may change the target while the student chases it.

KL Direction and State Distribution Are Independent

Two design axes are often conflated.

Divergence direction

Forward KL uses teacher probability as its local weighting:

\[ \operatorname{KL}(q\|p_\theta) = \mathbb E_{a\sim q} \left[\log q(a)-\log p_\theta(a)\right]. \]

It encourages the student to cover alternatives supported by the teacher. Reverse KL uses student probability as its local weighting:

\[ \operatorname{KL}(p_\theta\|q) = \mathbb E_{a\sim p_\theta} \left[\log p_\theta(a)-\log q(a)\right]. \]

It strongly discourages choices the student makes that the teacher considers unlikely, but can be mode-seeking. These are tendencies; capacity, support, temperature, and optimization also matter.

State or input distribution

The context \(h\) at which the distributions are compared can come from:

State source What it teaches Main risk
observed/data inputs behavior near the training distribution misses student-induced errors
teacher trajectories states a strong policy visits student may not reach them
student trajectories recovery on deployment-like states teacher may be unreliable off distribution
mixture or replay broader coverage weighting becomes another design choice

One can use forward or reverse KL under any of these state distributions. “Use reverse KL” does not by itself make an objective on-policy.

On-Policy Distillation for Autoregressive Models

Teacher forcing trains on data prefixes, whereas generation visits prefixes created by the student. On-policy distillation lets the student generate a prefix \(h_t\) and asks the teacher to evaluate that encountered state.

A common local objective is

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{OPD}}(h_t) = \operatorname{KL} \left( p_\theta(\cdot\mid h_t) \middle\| q(\cdot\mid h_t) \right). \]

For a sampled action \(a_t\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid h_t)\),

\[ r_t = \log q(a_t\mid h_t) - \log p_\theta(a_t\mid h_t) \]

can act as a dense token-level reward. Full-vocabulary KL is available when teacher logits can be queried; sampled implementations require care because the sampling distribution itself depends on \(\theta\).

On-policy training addresses state-distribution mismatch. Reverse KL chooses the local discrepancy geometry. They solve different problems.

Potential failures include:

  • the student reaches prefixes outside the teacher’s reliable region
  • reverse KL removes useful diversity
  • teacher querying dominates training cost
  • sampled gradient estimates have high variance
  • locally imitating next-token probabilities does not preserve sequence-level behavior

On-Policy Distillation develops the state- distribution view. MiniLLM studies reverse-KL distillation for generative language models. More recent trust-region methods such as TrOPD restrict unreliable updates and can mix forward-KL guidance into uncertain regions.

Sequence Distillation

When teacher logits are unavailable or expensive to store, generate teacher outputs \(\widetilde y\sim q(\cdot\mid x)\) and train by ordinary NLL:

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{seq}} = -\log p_\theta(\widetilde y\mid x). \]

This converts the teacher into a data generator. It is simple and works across different APIs or vocabularies, but a sampled sequence exposes far less information than the full teacher distribution. Decoding also changes the target: greedy outputs reduce diversity, while sampled outputs introduce variance and possible errors.

For reasoning models, a teacher can supply final answers, rationales, tool-use traces, or verified trajectories. These are different supervisory objects. Imitating a rationale’s tokens does not prove that the student has learned the same internal algorithm, and incorrect rationales can transfer persuasive failure modes.

Feature and Relational Distillation

Output matching is not the only transfer channel.

Feature matching

Given teacher and student representations \(h^T(x)\) and \(h^S(x)\),

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{feature}} = \left\|P_\phi(h^S(x))-h^T(x)\right\|_2^2, \]

where \(P_\phi\) aligns dimensions. Cosine distance or attention-map matching may be used instead. Layer alignment is an architectural hypothesis, not a neutral implementation detail: a smaller student may organize computation differently.

Relational matching

Rather than matching coordinates, match relations within a batch:

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{relation}} = \sum_{i,j} \left( \operatorname{sim}(h_i^S,h_j^S) - \operatorname{sim}(h_i^T,h_j^T) \right)^2. \]

This preserves teacher geometry while allowing rotations or different feature bases. It connects distillation to Representation Learning.

Self-distillation methods such as BYOL- and DINO-style training use a momentum or stop-gradient teacher to create a stable target. Their main challenge is not compression but preventing representational collapse.

Choosing the Distillation Objective

Constraint or goal Natural starting point
same label/token space and teacher logits available temperature-scaled forward KL
generation errors compound on student prefixes on-policy state collection
only teacher API outputs available sequence distillation
output vocabularies differ sequence-, feature-, or representation-level transfer
internal geometry matters feature or relational matching
teacher has multiple useful modes prefer covering objectives and audit diversity
deployment behavior matters more than token agreement trajectory or policy distillation

Common Failure Modes

Failure First-principles diagnosis
student copies teacher errors the teacher was treated as ground truth
no gain over hard labels temperature or teacher distribution exposes little extra information
capacity gap the student cannot represent the teacher target
mode loss divergence, decoding, or synthetic data collapses alternatives
exposure bias matching occurs only on data/teacher states
unstable on-policy learning student visits states where teacher guidance or estimation is unreliable
feature conflict forced layer correspondence is inappropriate
hidden cost teacher inference or stored logits erase the intended efficiency gain

Interview Questions

  • Why is distillation a supervision mechanism rather than one loss formula?
  • Derive why forward KL and soft-label cross-entropy have the same student gradient.
  • Why is the \(\tau^2\) factor commonly used in logit distillation?
  • Separate KL direction from the distribution over prefixes or inputs.
  • Why might reverse KL improve generation while reducing diversity?
  • Compare logit, sequence, feature, relational, and policy distillation.
  • When can sequence distillation work across different tokenizers?
  • How can a self-distillation teacher avoid becoming a moving, collapsing target?
