Senior-level scope and leadership

Named in 98% of 2253 senior postings · 30 review cards

The behavioural, hiring-manager and project deep-dive rounds, which decide the level of an offer more than the coding round does. Nearly every posting asks for ownership (about 78%), cross-functional work and communication (about 60% each), and half ask for comfort with ambiguity. Interviewers look for evidence that you chose the right problem, made the decisions, measured the result and changed what other people did. The cards here are guidance on what is being tested and how a strong answer is built; the stories must be your own.

The senior bar

What separates a senior answer from a mid-level one in this area.

  • Leads with the decision and the measured result, then explains the alternatives that were rejected and why
  • Separates “I” from “we” precisely and can go three levels deep on any decision in their own project
  • Shows judgement about what not to do, including stopping their own work, saying no and choosing the simple solution
  • Frames a business request as an action, a prediction target, a label source and a metric before proposing a model
  • Handles disagreement on substance by naming the other side’s position fairly, finding the crux and settling it with evidence
  • Gives evidence of leverage beyond their own output, such as people grown, standards adopted or other teams’ decisions changed
  • Talks about failure and uncertainty plainly, without blame and without hedging everything

corebehaviouralMLE AI RE Walk me through the most significant project you have owned. (This is the project deep-dive; how do you structure it?)

What is tested. Whether you made the decisions, whether you understand them below the surface, and whether the result was real. It is the round that most often sets the level.

Structure.

  1. Headline (20 s). The problem, your role, the result: one sentence each.
  2. Context (60 to 90 s). Why it mattered and to whom, the baseline, the constraints (latency, data, team size, deadline).
  3. Your decisions. Two or three that shaped the outcome, each as options considered, criteria, choice, evidence. Say “I” for what you decided and “we” for what the team did.
  4. Alternatives rejected. Why, given what was known then. Include the simple baseline.
  5. The hard part. What went wrong or surprised you, and how you found out.
  6. Measured result. Metric, baseline, delta, how it was measured (offline set, A/B test, interval), and what did not improve.
  7. Retrospective. What you would do differently and what it changed in your later work.

Delivery. Prepare a two-minute and a seven-minute version, and expect to be interrupted; follow the interviewer’s questions instead of finishing your script. Be ready to draw the system.

Choosing the project. Recent, you made the key calls, it has a measurable outcome, it has technical depth, and it is relevant to the role. A smaller project you drove beats a famous one you assisted.

Mistakes.

  • Five minutes of context before any decision.
  • An architecture tour with no choices in it.
  • No numbers. If they are confidential, give relative changes and keep the reasoning.
  • Unable to explain a central component that someone else built.
  • No failure or regret anywhere, which reads as low self-awareness.

Follow-ups

  • What was the simplest baseline, and how much of the gain came on top of it?
  • If you started again today, what would you build differently?
seniorbehaviouralMLE AI RE In a deep-dive the interviewer keeps asking “why?” and “what exactly did you do?”. What are they probing for, and how do you prepare for it?

What the questions map to.

  • “What exactly did you do?”: authorship. Were the decisions yours?
  • “Why that and not X?”: judgement. Did you consider alternatives, and on what criteria?
  • “How do you know it worked?”: rigour. Baseline, noise, leakage, confounders, a fair comparison.
  • “How does that component work?”: depth below the abstraction you used.
  • “What would break at 10x?” and “what would you change?”: whether you see the limits of your own design.

Preparation, for two or three projects.

  • A decision log: each key decision with its options, the reason, the evidence, and what you would decide today.
  • A numbers sheet: data size, label counts, model size, training cost, QPS, latency, metric values with baselines, evaluation set size, experiment duration, team size.
  • The architecture drawn from memory.
  • The ten hardest questions, answered aloud. Typical ones: why not a simpler baseline, how do you know the gain came from your change, what broke in production, what did the evaluation not cover.
  • The basics of adjacent components you depended on but did not build.

In the room.

  • Answer at the level asked, in one or two sentences, then offer depth.
  • When you do not know: say so, say who owned it, give your understanding and how you would check. Bluffing is usually detected, and it costs more than the gap.
  • Do not defend every past decision. “That was wrong; here is what I learned” is a senior answer.
  • If pushed on a design choice, treat it as a discussion: restate the concern, say what holds and what does not.

Mistakes. Answering a different question, drowning a simple question in detail, becoming defensive, and results stated without a baseline.

Follow-ups

  • How do you know the improvement was caused by your change and not by something else that shipped?
  • Which part of that system do you understand least?
corebehaviouralMLE AI RE Tell me about a time you failed, or a project that did not work.

What is tested. Ownership without blame, honesty, and whether the lesson changed your behaviour.

Choosing the story. A real failure with a consequence, where your own decision contributed. Not a success in disguise, and not negligence that would worry a hiring manager. Typical honest ML failures: an offline gain that did not transfer online, leakage that inflated results, a quarter of modelling when the data was the problem, a launch without a guardrail metric, underestimated integration work.

Structure.

  1. Context, briefly.
  2. The decision you made and why it looked reasonable then.
  3. What happened and how it was detected (best if you detected it).
  4. What you did immediately: mitigate, inform the people affected.
  5. The root cause, including your part in it.
  6. What you changed: a concrete habit or process.
  7. Evidence that the change held: a later situation where it mattered.

Spend about half the time on steps 4 to 7.

Mistakes.

  • Blaming the data, the PM, the deadline or another team.
  • A generic lesson such as “communicate more”.
  • A failure too small to matter.
  • Still sounding bitter.
  • Claiming you have none. In ML most ideas fail; no failures suggests no measurement.

Follow-ups

  • What was the earliest signal you missed?
  • What do you do differently now as a direct result?
seniorbehaviouralMLE AI You launched a model and a key metric regressed. Walk me through the first hour, the first day and the first week.

First hour: contain.

  • Is it real, and is it ours? Check the size against normal noise, the timing against the rollout, and which segments moved.
  • If a guardrail is breached and the link is plausible, roll back or ramp down first. It is cheap and reversible; diagnosis can continue on a small slice or offline.
  • Send one message to stakeholders: what we see, what we did, when the next update comes. They should hear it from you.

First day: diagnose. Work through the causes in order of cost to check:

  • Experiment validity: sample-ratio mismatch, a logging change, an overlapping launch.
  • Training-serving skew: feature parity, preprocessing or tokenizer version, stale or missing features, default values.
  • A latency regression: a slower model lowers engagement by itself.
  • Proxy mismatch: the offline metric improved while the product metric fell (for example clicks up, retention down).
  • Distribution: the offline evaluation set does not represent live traffic; check slices such as new users, locale and platform.

First week: fix the system.

  • A blameless write-up: timeline, impact (quantified), root cause, why it was not caught earlier.
  • Actions with owners: the offline test that would have caught it, a new guardrail, a shadow or canary stage, a parity check.
  • A decision on relaunch, with criteria.

What interviewers listen for. Rollback before debugging, communication without being asked, quantified impact, and a fix to the process as well as to the bug.

Mistakes. Debugging in production while the regression continues; a silent rollback; attributing it to noise without checking; blaming the experiment platform or another team.

Follow-ups

  • The offline metric was clearly better. Give three reasons the online metric could still fall.
  • When would you not roll back?
corebehaviouralMLE AI RE What does end-to-end ownership mean for an ML engineer? Give an example of something you did outside your formal remit because the outcome needed it.

Definition. Ownership is accountability for the outcome (the user or business metric), not for the task (a model was trained). For ML it spans the whole chain: problem definition, data, model, evaluation, serving, launch, monitoring, iteration, and retirement.

Behaviours interviewers look for.

  • Notices and closes gaps that fall between teams: a labelling bug, a logging error, a broken dashboard.
  • Stays after launch: watches the metrics, carries the pager, does the post-launch analysis.
  • Raises risks early and says what is not known.
  • Follows a problem to resolution; filing a ticket is not the end.
  • Decides reversible matters within their remit and informs others, and escalates what is irreversible, cross-team or outside their expertise.
  • Cleans up: documentation, dead experiments, unused compute.

Structure of the example.

  1. The gap you noticed.
  2. Why it was nobody’s job.
  3. What you did, including who you informed.
  4. The result.
  5. The systemic fix, so that it does not depend on heroics next time.

The hiring manager’s underlying question is: can I hand this person a problem and not need to check?

Mistakes.

  • A hero story that reveals poor planning.
  • Overriding another team instead of working with them.
  • Ownership described as long hours.
  • Ownership as control: unable to delegate.
  • A list of responsibilities instead of an outcome.

Follow-ups

  • Tell me about something you owned that failed after you handed it over.
  • Where is the line between ownership and stepping on another team’s remit?

seniorbehaviouralMLE AI RE Tell me about a disagreement with a researcher or a product manager. How was it resolved?

What is tested. Whether you can disagree on substance and keep the working relationship, whether you look for the other person’s real constraint, and whether you commit once a decision is made.

Common forms in ML teams.

  • A researcher wants more time or a more novel method; you want to ship the baseline.
  • A PM wants a date or feature that the model’s quality does not support.
  • A PM wants to optimise a metric you believe is gameable.
  • Offline results look strong, but latency or cost rules the model out in production.

Structure.

  1. State their position fairly, in terms they would accept.
  2. Say what was at stake.
  3. What you did: raised it one-to-one first; found the crux (a disagreement about facts, about priorities, or about risk); proposed the cheapest experiment or data that would change either mind; agreed the decision criterion before seeing the result; set a time box.
  4. The outcome, including what you conceded.
  5. The relationship afterwards and what you now do differently.

If no agreement is reached, escalate together, with both positions written down, to the person who owns the decision. Then commit to the decision and do not relitigate it.

Stronger stories include one where you were wrong or partly wrong, or where the resolution was a third option.

Mistakes.

  • The other person is portrayed as unreasonable.
  • You were simply right and they came round.
  • Winning by going around them.
  • Never having disagreed, which reads as low conviction.
  • A story about personalities, not substance.

Follow-ups

  • What was their strongest argument?
  • Tell me about a time you lost the argument. What did you do next?
seniorbehaviouralAI MLE You are embedded with a customer who expects an AI feature in production in six weeks. Their data is messy and their expectations come from a demo. How do you run the engagement?

What is tested. Technical judgement, empathy for the customer, and commercial sense, together.

Discovery (week 1).

  • Who pays, who uses it, which workflow, and what success means in their terms (hours saved, error rate).
  • What “production” means to them: number of users, availability, security review.
  • Collect real examples of inputs and desired outputs. They become the evaluation set.
  • Start access, security and data-privacy requests on the first day; they are usually the critical path.

Reset expectations with evidence. Run the system on 50 of their own examples and show the failure categories. Then agree written acceptance criteria: a metric on an agreed test set and a defined scope (which document types, which languages), not “works well”.

Deliver a thin slice. One workflow, with a human reviewing outputs, working end to end in about two weeks. Iterate weekly against the evaluation set, and show the numbers.

Communicate. A weekly written status: done, numbers, risks, requests. One named counterpart. Answer scope additions with a trade, not a flat refusal.

Feed the product. Keep customer-specific configuration separate from product code. Bring general gaps back to the product team with evidence of frequency and value, and do not fork the product quietly.

Hand over. Monitoring, a feedback channel, a runbook, an evaluation set the customer owns, user training.

Hard moments.

  • They demand accuracy that cannot be reached: design the handling of the remaining errors (a review queue) instead of promising a number.
  • Their data cannot support the use case: say so early, with evidence and options.

Mistakes. Promising to please, disappearing to build for three weeks, treating the customer as an obstacle, and shipping a one-off that nobody can maintain.

Follow-ups

  • The customer’s champion asks you to commit to 99% accuracy in writing. What do you say?
  • How do you decide whether a customer request belongs in the product?
seniorbehaviouralMLE AI RE A product manager asks when the model will reach the target quality. How do you plan and communicate ML work whose outcome is uncertain?

What is tested. Whether partners can plan around you. The two failing answers are a padded single date and “it is research, so it cannot be estimated”.

Approach.

  • Separate what can be estimated from what cannot. Pipelines, serving and evaluation infrastructure are engineering and get dates. Whether a metric reaches a target is uncertain and gets probabilities and decision points.
  • Plan as time-boxed questions. For example: by week 2 a baseline and an evaluation show the size of the gap; by week 4 a headroom analysis shows whether this approach can close it; at week 8 a go or no-go. Commit to the dates of the decisions, not to the result.
  • Give ranges with the main dependency, update them at each milestone, and report a slip when you learn of it, not at the deadline.
  • Take the research bet off the launch’s critical path. Offer fallbacks: ship the baseline, restrict to high-confidence cases, or keep a human in the loop.
  • Make quality visible. Share examples of failures, not only a number, and define “good enough” together from the cost of errors.
  • Ask about their flexibility. The target, the scope or the date can often move if you explain what each buys.

With researchers. Agree what a hand-off contains (a reproducible checkpoint, the evaluation, inference cost), and bring latency and cost limits in at the start, not after the result.

Mistakes. A confident date for an uncertain outcome, silence until the deadline, promising a metric, and treating the question as an imposition.

Follow-ups

  • The week-4 analysis says the target is out of reach. How do you tell the PM?
  • How do you estimate the probability that an approach will work?

seniordesignMLE AI RE What goes into a design doc for an ML system, and what makes reviewers trust it?

Sections.

  1. Summary. The recommendation and the decision you need, in a few lines.
  2. Problem and context. Who is affected, why now, the link to a business metric.
  3. Goals and non-goals. Non-goals prevent most scope arguments.
  4. Success metrics. Offline, online and guardrails, with target values.
  5. Current state. The existing system and baseline numbers.
  6. Proposed design. Data (sources, labels, volume, freshness, privacy), model, training, evaluation plan, serving (latency and cost budget, capacity), rollout (shadow, canary, A/B), monitoring, fallback.
  7. Alternatives considered. Include “do nothing” and the simplest non-ML option, each with the reason it was rejected.
  8. Risks and open questions. Including what result would make you stop.
  9. Plan. Milestones with decision points, cost in people and compute, dependencies and requests of other teams.

What earns trust.

  • Numbers with sources, and assumptions stated as assumptions.
  • Honest alternatives, not strawmen.
  • Risks named by the author before a reviewer finds them.
  • Written for a sceptical reader from another team: terms defined, a diagram, no unexplained internal names.
  • Length matched to the decision; two to six pages is typical.
  • Circulated early to the likely critics; decisions and dissent recorded in the doc.

Mistakes.

  • A description of a solution with no problem statement.
  • No metric, or a metric without a target.
  • Written after the system is built, as justification.
  • No explicit request of the reader.
  • Left stale after the design changed.

Follow-ups

  • How is a design doc for an ML system different from one for a backend service?
  • A reviewer disagrees with your core choice in the comments. What do you do?
coreconceptMLE AI RE How do you write up an experiment result so that a busy reader reaches the right conclusion?

Put the conclusion first and make every claim checkable.

Structure.

  1. Headline. The conclusion and recommendation in one or two sentences: ship, do not ship, or inconclusive and why.
  2. What was tested. The hypothesis, the single change, the baseline.
  3. Setup. Data or traffic, duration, sample size, metric definitions, and the primary metric chosen in advance.
  4. Results. A table with baseline, treatment, absolute and relative delta, and a confidence interval or seed-to-seed spread. Then guardrails, then the slices where the effect differs.
  5. Interpretation. The likely mechanism, and the alternative explanations you ruled out.
  6. Caveats. Novelty effects, sample-ratio mismatch, multiple comparisons, overlap between evaluation and training data, anything not measured.
  7. Next steps. The decision requested and the follow-up.
  8. Reproducibility. Commit, config, data snapshot, run ids.

Rules.

  • Report null and negative results with the same care.
  • Give both forms of a delta: “+0.4 points (1.2% relative)”.
  • Give intervals and effect sizes, not only “significant”.
  • State the noise floor (an A/A test, or the spread across seeds), so the reader can judge a small gain.
  • One chart per claim, with labelled axes and a visible baseline.
  • Mark slices found after the fact as exploratory.
  • Keep observation (“metric X rose”) separate from inference (“because of Y”).

Mistakes. The conclusion on page three, ten metrics and no primary one, a gain with no baseline, selective slices, and a write-up that cannot be reproduced.

Follow-ups

  • The primary metric is flat and one slice shows a large gain. How do you report it?
  • What do you write when the result is inconclusive?
corebehaviouralMLE AI RE How do you explain a technical decision or a model’s limitation to a non-technical executive or customer?

What is tested. In the interview this is often tested directly: “explain your project to me as if I were in sales”. Signals are structure, absence of jargon, and whether you check understanding.

Approach.

  1. Start from their decision. What do they need to decide or do after hearing you?
  2. Lead with the conclusion and the request.
  3. Use their units: money, time, risk, customers affected. Replace “precision 0.7, recall 0.9” with counts: “of every 100 transactions we flag, about 70 are fraud, and we miss about 1 fraud in 10” (illustrative numbers).
  4. State uncertainty as a range and name what it depends on.
  5. Offer options with their trade-offs and give a recommendation.
  6. For a limitation, cover three things: what it cannot do, what the user sees when it fails, and what the mitigation is.
  7. Check understanding by asking what they will tell their own team, and follow up in writing.

Prepare three depths of the same explanation: 30 seconds, 2 minutes and 10 minutes. Use at most one analogy, and say where it stops being accurate.

Mistakes.

  • Telling the story in chronological order.
  • Hedging every sentence until there is no message.
  • Hiding bad news inside detail.
  • Talking down to the listener.
  • Promising certainty to end the conversation.

Follow-ups

  • Explain overfitting to a product manager in three sentences.
  • The executive asks “so is it 95% accurate?” and the honest answer is “it depends on the customer”. What do you say?
corebehaviouralMLE AI RE Why this company, and why are you leaving your current role?

What is tested. Whether your motivation is specific and durable (will you accept, stay and care), and whether there are warning signs such as bitterness or indifference to the work.

Why this company: three parts, 60 to 90 seconds.

  1. The problem or product, specifically. Something you have used or read (the product’s behaviour, an engineering post, a paper), and an opinion about it.
  2. The fit. What you have done that maps onto what they need now.
  3. The growth. What you want to learn or own that this place is unusually suited for.

Test: if the answer could be given unchanged to a competitor, it is too generic.

Why leaving.

  • Describe what you are moving toward: scope, problem, stage, closeness to research or to users.
  • One sentence on what is missing today, without blame.
  • Keep it true and short. If it was a layoff or a reorganisation, say so plainly.
  • Do not criticise your manager or company, and do not disclose confidential details.

Consistency. The answer must agree with what you say you want in other rounds, with the questions you ask, and with the level you are interviewing for.

At senior level, add what you would want to take on in the first year. It shows you have thought about their problems, not only your career.

Mistakes. Flattery, “looking for a new challenge”, a long career history, complaints, and sounding as if any offer would do.

Follow-ups

  • What would make you stay at your current company?
  • What concerns do you have about this role?
corebehaviouralMLE AI RE What questions do you ask your interviewers, and what do your questions signal?

Questions serve two purposes: information for your own decision, and a signal of level, since they show what you think matters. Prepare two or three per interviewer, and prefer a follow-up to something they said.

Hiring manager.

  • What does success look like in this role at 6 and 12 months?
  • What is the hardest problem the team has now?
  • How are priorities set, and what was deprioritised last?
  • What distinguishes senior from staff here?
  • Why is the role open?

Engineers.

  • How does an idea get from proposal to production, and how long does that take?
  • What do the evaluation and experiment tools look like, and what is painful?
  • How is time split between new work and maintenance? What is on-call like?
  • How does the team use coding agents, and what are the review norms?

Researchers.

  • How is work divided between research and engineering?
  • How is compute allocated?
  • How are research bets chosen and stopped?

Product and other partners.

  • Which metric is the team judged by?
  • How are quality, cost and latency trade-offs decided?

Senior leaders.

  • What is the largest risk to the strategy over two years?

Reading the answers. Warning signs: no clear metric, no evaluation, “we will work out the scope later”, vagueness about why the last person left.

Mistakes.

  • No questions.
  • Questions answered on the careers page.
  • Compensation and logistics put to an engineer; those go to the recruiter.
  • Asking only about promotion.
  • Not listening to the answer.

Follow-ups

  • What would an answer have to contain for you to decline the offer?

staffconceptMLE AI RE What makes a story staff-level rather than senior-level? Describe how the two versions of the same project would differ.

The difference is scope, ambiguity and influence, not technical difficulty alone.

Dimension Senior Staff
Scope A project or system inside one team A problem across teams, or a multi-quarter area
Origin Solves a problem that was handed over, possibly loosely defined Finds and defines the problem and convinces others it matters
Ambiguity The solution is unclear The goal and direction are unclear
Influence Leads by doing; directs a few engineers Changes what other teams build, without authority
Leverage Own output plus mentees Platforms, standards and decisions that multiply others
Horizon A quarter or two A year or more, with second-order effects considered
Trade-offs Technical Technical, organisational and business: staffing, sequencing, what not to do
Evidence A metric moved The metric, plus adoption by others, decisions changed, people grown

An illustration of the same work told twice. Senior: “I built a new retrieval model and recall improved.” Staff: “Three teams were building separate retrieval stacks with incompatible evaluations. I wrote the proposal for a shared evaluation and index, got two sceptical teams to agree, led the first migration, and the others migrated without me.”

Interviewers verify a staff claim with specifics:

  • Who disagreed, and what did you do about it?
  • What did you decide not to do?
  • What happened after you stepped away?

Mistakes. Using larger words for the same scope, claiming organisational impact that was your manager’s, and presenting heroic individual execution as leadership. Very strong solo delivery reads as “strong senior”.

Follow-ups

  • Which of your projects would still have happened if you had not been there?
  • Tell me about a technical direction you set that others followed. Who resisted?
seniorbehaviouralMLE AI RE Describe a time you needed another team to do something and had no authority over them.

What is tested. Your mechanism of influence. “I explained and they agreed” is not a mechanism.

Structure.

seniordesignMLE AI You join as the first ML engineer on a new product. There is no data pipeline, no evaluation and no model. How do you sequence the first 90 days?

Build a thin end-to-end slice first, and build the evaluation before iterating on the model.

Weeks 0 to 2: understand and ship version zero.

  • Learn the product and the user; agree the task and the success metric with product.
  • Find what data exists.
  • Ship the simplest thing that works end to end: a heuristic, or a hosted model behind a prompt.
  • Log inputs, outputs and user actions from the first day. Without logs there is no data to improve on.

Weeks 2 to 4: evaluation.

  • A few hundred labelled examples drawn from real usage, with the important slices.
  • An error taxonomy and baseline numbers.
  • One command that produces the numbers in minutes.

Weeks 4 to 8: iterate on the largest error bucket.

  • Simple methods first: prompt and retrieval changes, a small fine-tune, a gradient-boosted model.
  • Release behind a flag to real users, with a fallback and a latency and cost budget.

Weeks 8 to 12: measure online and plan.

  • A staged rollout or A/B test, monitoring, a feedback loop from users into the evaluation set.
  • Decide what to own next. Moving from a hosted API to your own model is justified by volume and unit cost, latency, privacy or domain quality, not by preference.

Principles.

  • Borrow infrastructure. A feature store, a custom training platform or a cluster is rarely needed in the first quarter.
  • Decide reversible things quickly; slow down for the irreversible ones (data schema, labelling policy, privacy commitments).
  • Take on technical debt deliberately and keep a list.

Mistakes. Starting with model architecture, having no logging, iterating by impression with no evaluation set, and polishing before anyone uses it.

Follow-ups

  • What would you deliberately not build in the first quarter?
  • How do you get labels when there are no users yet?

coretrade-offMLE AI RE When should you not use ML for a problem?

Do not use it when a cheaper, deterministic solution meets the requirement, or when you cannot tell whether the model works.

Conditions against ML.

  • Rules, a lookup or a query are good enough. They are deterministic, debuggable and nearly free to run.
  • No data, no labels, no feedback signal, and no realistic way to get them.
  • Errors are intolerable and unreviewed. No human check, no fallback, no undo.
  • Exact or explainable behaviour is required, by regulation or contract.
  • It cannot pay back. Roughly, value per decision x number of decisions x expected lift must exceed the cost of building, serving, monitoring and maintaining. Low-volume decisions rarely qualify.
  • The effect cannot be measured, so nobody can tell an improvement from a regression.
  • The bottleneck is elsewhere: the interface, the inventory, the latency.

Costs that are easy to forget. Monitoring, drift, retraining, upstream data dependencies, on-call, and feedback loops between the model and its own training data.

Practice. Start with a heuristic baseline. It becomes the benchmark and later the fallback, and ML has to beat it by a margin the product can feel.

The current version of the same question.

  • Do not use an LLM where a regular expression, a small classifier or plain code works: cost, latency and nondeterminism all get worse.
  • Do not use an agent where a fixed workflow works.
  • Do not fine-tune where a prompt with retrieval is sufficient.

seniortrade-offMLE AI RE How do you choose the metric for an ML product? Give an example where optimising the obvious metric hurts the product.

Build the chain from the business goal down, and check each link, because each is a proxy for the one above.

  1. Business goal: retention, revenue, tasks completed.
  2. Online product metric: sensitive enough to move within an experiment.
  3. Offline metric: computed on a fixed set; useful only if offline gains have historically predicted online gains.
  4. Training loss: what the optimiser sees.

A good metric is aligned with user value, sensitive, available quickly, hard to game, and defined precisely (unit of analysis, time window, denominator).

Guardrails and counter-metrics. Every target needs the metric that would reveal gaming: latency, cost, complaint or harm rate, diversity, long-term retention, performance on important slices.

Examples of the obvious metric failing.

  • Click-through rate rewards clickbait: clicks rise, satisfaction and return visits fall.
  • Watch time rewards content people regret watching.
  • Deflection rate for a support bot rises when users give up. Pair it with re-contact within 7 days and confirmed resolution.
  • Suggestion acceptance rate for a code assistant rises with short, trivial suggestions. Measure the code that is still there later.
  • Fraud recall at a fixed false-positive rate ignores amounts. Weight by value.
  • An LLM-judge score can reward length.

This is Goodhart’s law: a measure that becomes a target stops measuring.

Process. Write the definition down, read examples from both ends of the metric to confirm it means what you think, declare one primary metric and two to four guardrails before the experiment, and agree the decision rule.

Follow-ups

  • Your offline metric improves and the online metric does not. What do you investigate?
  • How do you measure long-term effects in a two-week experiment?
seniortrade-offMLE AI RE How does the cost of errors change your design? Take a classifier whose false positives and false negatives cost very different amounts.

Threshold from costs. With a calibrated probability \(p\) of the positive class and no cost for correct decisions, act when the expected cost of not acting is larger:

\[p\,C_{FN} > (1-p)\,C_{FP} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p > \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}}.\]

If a missed fraud costs 100 units and a blocked good customer costs 5, the threshold is \(5/105 \approx 0.048\), far from 0.5. When the cost varies per case (the transaction amount), compare expected costs per case instead of using one threshold. This only works if \(p\) is calibrated.

More than two actions. Allow, ask for extra verification, send to manual review, block. The uncertain band goes to the cheaper intermediate action. Review capacity is a constraint: if reviewers handle 500 cases a day, the operating point is precision in the top 500.

Let the product absorb errors.

  • Suggest instead of acting automatically.
  • Make actions undoable and ask for confirmation before irreversible ones.
  • Show sources or confidence where users can act on them.
  • Provide a fallback path when confidence is low.

Evaluate accordingly. Cost-weighted metrics at the operating point, not AUC; calibration; the slices where the cost concentrates.

Rare catastrophic errors are not handled by averages. Use hard constraints: rules, allowlists, human approval.

Feedback effect. Blocked cases never produce a label, so the training data is biased by past decisions. Keep a small randomly approved sample.

For LLM features. The acceptable error rate depends on reversibility and review: a drafted email that a human edits is cheap to get wrong; an executed refund is not. Set the level of autonomy by error cost.

Follow-ups

  • Your probabilities are not calibrated. What changes?
  • How do you estimate the cost of a false positive that annoys a customer?

coredesignMLE AI RE A stakeholder says “we want to reduce churn with AI”. How do you turn that into an ML problem? Give your general procedure.
  1. Action. What will be done differently with a prediction, by whom, and when? Here: a retention offer, or a call from an account manager. No available action means no project.
  2. Value. The current churn rate, what a point of churn is worth, and what the action costs.
  3. Target. A precise label, unit, horizon and prediction time: “will this active subscriber cancel within 30 days, scored weekly, using data up to the scoring date”. Check that the label is observable and when it arrives.
  4. Is prediction the right formulation? The goal is to reduce churn, not to predict it. The customers worth contacting are the ones the action changes (uplift), not the ones with the highest risk: some will leave anyway, and some are reminded to cancel. Uplift needs data from a randomised intervention, so start with a risk model plus a random holdout that measures the incremental effect.
  5. Data. Which features exist at prediction time, how much history, how many positives. Remove leakage, such as a “cancellation requested” flag.
  6. Metrics. Offline: lift in the top decile, PR-AUC, calibration. Online: retention against the holdout, net of the offer’s cost. Guardrails: margin, complaints.
  7. Constraints. Batch scoring is acceptable; the account team can make 200 calls a week, so precision in the top 200 is the operating point; reasons must be shown to the caller; privacy rules apply.
  8. Baseline. A rule (“no login for 14 days”), then a gradient-boosted model.
  9. Agreement. Success and stop criteria, written down with the stakeholder.

Mistakes. Accepting the framing as given, predicting something nobody can act on, optimising AUC, ignoring the capacity of the intervention, and leakage from post-outcome fields.

  • Why can targeting the highest-risk customers reduce retention?
  • The label arrives 30 days late. How does that affect training and monitoring?
seniordesignMLE AI RE Frame each of these as an ML problem in about two minutes each: “cut support costs with an LLM”, “stop fraud losses”, “make search better”.

Cut support costs.

  • Action: which contacts are answered automatically, which are drafted for an agent, which are routed.

  • Data: past tickets with resolutions, and the knowledge base.
  • Metric: contacts resolved without a human and without re-contact within N days, plus satisfaction and cost per resolved contact. Guardrail: wrong-answer rate on an audited sample.
  • Trap: the most frequent intents often need an action (a refund, an address change), so the limit is tool integration and authorisation, not text quality. The knowledge base sets the ceiling.

Stop fraud losses.

  • Action: allow, challenge, review or block, at transaction time, within a budget of tens of milliseconds.
  • Formulation: cost-sensitive binary classification, with anomaly detection for new patterns.
  • Labels: chargebacks arrive weeks later, exist only for transactions that were allowed, and attackers adapt.
  • Metric: value-weighted recall at a fixed approval rate. The business number is fraud loss plus lost good revenue plus review cost.
  • Trap: a random split leaks across time and across entities. Split by time.

  • First diagnose: sample failed sessions (no click, reformulation, abandonment) and bucket them into recall failure, ranking failure, query understanding, missing inventory, interface.
  • Formulation follows the largest bucket: retrieval or query rewriting, learning to rank, spelling and intent models. Missing inventory is not an ML problem.
  • Metric: offline NDCG@k and recall@k on judged queries; online session success and reformulation rate; latency as a guardrail.
  • Trap: click labels carry position bias, and “better” has no meaning until the success event is defined.

  • For the support case, how would you prove that the cost reduction is real?
  • In the fraud case, how do you get labels for transactions you blocked?
