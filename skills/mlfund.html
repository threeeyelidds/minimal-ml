The senior bar

What separates a senior answer from a mid-level one in this area.

  • Derives the standard results on a whiteboard (logistic loss and gradient, the XGBoost split gain, the downsampling correction, a sample-size formula) and says what each implies in practice.
  • Treats evaluation as a design problem. Chooses the split unit and time gap that match deployment, names the leakage paths, and puts an interval on every reported number.
  • Separates ranking quality from calibration and from the decision threshold, and knows which of the three a given product needs.
  • Knows what the labels really are. Asks about delay, selection by the previous model, annotator agreement and sampling before trusting a metric.
  • Reaches for the simple strong baseline first (regularised linear model, tuned GBDT, seasonal naive) and can say when it stops being enough.
  • States the assumptions behind a statistical or causal claim, and what would falsify them, instead of quoting a p-value.

coreconceptMLE AI REasked at Nvidia, Amazon Explain the bias-variance decomposition. In practice, how do you tell which of the two is limiting a model, and what do you change in each case?

For squared loss at a point \(x\), with \(y = f(x) + \varepsilon\), \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]=0\), \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2\), and the expectation taken over training sets \(D\):

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[(y-\hat f_D(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}_D[\hat f_D(x)]-f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}_D[\hat f_D(x)]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}} \]

Diagnosis comes from learning curves, not from the formula:

  • High bias: training and validation error are both high and close together; more data does not help.
  • High variance: a large train-validation gap; validation error keeps improving with more data; results move a lot across seeds or folds.

Fixes. Bias: more capacity, better features, less regularisation, longer training. Variance: more data, regularisation, early stopping, a simpler model, or averaging. Averaging \(M\) models with variance \(\sigma^2\) and pairwise correlation \(\rho\) gives variance \(\rho\sigma^2 + (1-\rho)\sigma^2/M\), which is why bagging works only as far as the models are decorrelated.

Caveats. The additive form is specific to squared loss. Variance is not monotone in parameter count for over-parameterised models (double descent). The noise term is a floor you should estimate, for example from agreement between duplicate labels, before chasing the last point of error.

coretrade-offMLE AI RE Compare L1 and L2 regularisation. Why does L1 produce exact zeros while L2 does not, and when would you choose each?

L2 adds \(\frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|_2^2\). Its gradient \(\lambda w\) shrinks each weight in proportion to its size, so weights get small but never reach zero. L1 adds \(\lambda\|w\|_1\). Its subgradient \(\lambda\,\mathrm{sign}(w)\) is a constant pull, so any weight whose loss gradient is smaller than \(\lambda\) in magnitude is held exactly at zero.

With an orthonormal design (\(X^\top X = I\), loss \(\frac12\|y-Xw\|^2\)) both have closed forms:

\[ \hat w_j^{\text{ridge}} = \frac{w_j^{\text{OLS}}}{1+\lambda}, \qquad \hat w_j^{\text{lasso}} = \mathrm{sign}(w_j^{\text{OLS}})\,\max\big(|w_j^{\text{OLS}}|-\lambda,\,0\big) \]

Ridge rescales; lasso soft-thresholds. Probabilistically, L2 is MAP with a Gaussian prior and L1 is MAP with a Laplace prior.

Choose L1 for feature selection and sparse serving models (FTRL-Proximal with L1 is the classic sparse CTR setup). It is unstable with correlated features, picking one of a group arbitrarily; elastic net fixes that.

Choose L2 when many features carry small effects or are correlated. It adds \(\lambda\) to every Hessian eigenvalue, so the problem becomes strongly convex and better conditioned.

Pitfalls. The penalty depends on feature scale, so standardise first. Do not penalise the bias. Plain SGD on L1 does not give exact zeros; use a proximal (soft-threshold) update. With Adam, an L2 term in the loss is not the same as weight decay (AdamW).

Follow-ups

  • Along a direction where \(X^\top X\) has eigenvalue \(s^2\), by how much does ridge shrink the OLS solution?
  • Why must features be standardised before regularising?
seniorconceptMLE RE Early stopping and dropout are both called regularisers. What is each one actually doing, and in what sense is dropout an approximate ensemble?

Early stopping limits how far the parameters travel from initialisation. For gradient descent from zero on a quadratic loss, the component of the solution along a Hessian eigen-direction with eigenvalue \(h\) is scaled by \(1-(1-\eta h)^t\) after \(t\) steps, while L2 scales it by \(h/(h+\lambda)\). The two agree approximately when \(\lambda \approx 1/(\eta t)\): low-curvature directions, the ones the data determines poorly, are learned last. In practice, monitor a validation metric with patience and restore the best checkpoint. The set used for stopping is no longer an unbiased estimate, so keep a separate test set.

Dropout samples a mask per step and trains a random sub-network; all \(2^n\) sub-networks share weights. Inverted dropout multiplies kept activations by \(1/(1-p)\) during training so that inference uses the full network unchanged. That single forward pass approximates averaging the sub-networks’ predictions: it is the exact normalised geometric mean for a single softmax layer and an approximation for deep non-linear networks. For linear regression, dropout on the inputs equals, in expectation, an L2 penalty weighted by each feature’s second moment.

Failure modes. Forgetting model.eval(). Dropout placed before BatchNorm shifts activation variance between training and inference. In large-data pretraining the model under-fits, so dropout is often set to zero. Keeping dropout on at inference (MC dropout) gives a cheap, rough uncertainty estimate.

Follow-ups

  • Why is the early-stopped validation score optimistic?
  • Why do networks trained on noisy labels benefit from early stopping in particular?
staffconceptMLE REasked at Anthropic What is double descent, and does it contradict the bias-variance trade-off? Be precise about what is established and what is not.

Test error as a function of capacity can fall, rise to a peak near the interpolation threshold (where the model can just fit the training set exactly), and fall again as capacity grows further. The same shape has been reported against training time (epoch-wise) and, near the threshold, against sample size.

It does not contradict the decomposition, which is an identity. It contradicts the assumption that variance grows monotonically with parameter count. Near the threshold there is essentially one interpolating solution and it must fit the noise, so variance spikes. Far beyond it there are many interpolating solutions and the optimiser’s implicit bias picks a low-norm one.

Linear regression makes this concrete. The minimum-norm least-squares solution is \(\hat w = X^{+}y\), and its variance contains \(\sum_i 1/s_i^2\) over the singular values of \(X\). When the number of features is close to \(n\) the smallest singular value approaches zero and the variance blows up.

Established: the phenomenon in deep networks (Belkin et al., 2019; Nakkiran et al., 2019), proofs for linear and random-feature models, a larger peak with more label noise, and that well-tuned regularisation (ridge, weight decay, early stopping) largely removes the peak.

Not established: that bigger is always better, or that parameter count is the right complexity measure. Practical reading: if validation error rises with model size, check regularisation and label noise before concluding the model is too big.

Follow-ups

  • Why does the minimum-norm solution generalise better than an arbitrary interpolating one?
  • Near the threshold, how can adding training data make test error worse?

seniordebuggingMLE AI REasked at OpenAI A model scores 0.93 AUC in 5-fold cross-validation and 0.71 in its first week in production. Walk through how you would find the cause.

A gap this large is usually leakage or a split that does not match deployment, not drift. I check in this order:

  1. Split unit. Random K-fold assumes independent rows. If the same user, patient, device or near-duplicate appears in train and validation, the model memorises the entity. Use group K-fold on the entity that will be new at serving time.
  2. Time. A random split trains on the future. Use a forward split with a gap at least as long as the label delay and the longest feature window.
  3. Feature leakage. Features computed after the label event, or from the label: a target encoding fit on all rows, an aggregate whose window includes the event, a status field updated after the outcome. Rank single-feature AUCs; an implausibly strong feature is the suspect. Compare feature timestamps with prediction time.
  4. Preprocessing fit on all data. Scalers, imputers, feature selection, PCA or oversampling applied before the split. Fit them inside each training fold.
  5. Selection on the reported folds. Hyperparameters, early stopping and thresholds tuned on the same folds that produced 0.93. Use nested CV or an untouched test set.
  6. Training-serving skew. The feature is computed differently online (defaults, freshness, units). Join logged serving features to offline features for the same ids and compare.
  7. Only then real shift: population, label definition, seasonality.

To confirm, rebuild the split to mimic deployment (time plus group). The offline score should drop towards 0.71; if it does not, the problem is on the serving side.

Follow-ups

  • When is plain K-fold fine?
  • How do you choose the gap in a temporal split?
  • Feature selection never looks at validation predictions. Why is doing it before the split still leakage?

seniorderivationMLE RE You train a CTR model on logs where negatives were downsampled to 10%. Derive the correction that restores calibrated probabilities, and say when you would prefer reweighting, or no resampling at all.

Keep every positive and each negative with probability \(s\). Sampling depends only on the label, so the class-conditional feature densities are unchanged and the odds in the sample are inflated by \(1/s\):

\[ \frac{p_s}{1-p_s}=\frac{1}{s}\cdot\frac{p}{1-p} \;\Rightarrow\; \operatorname{logit}(p)=\operatorname{logit}(p_s)+\log s, \qquad p=\frac{s\,p_s}{1-p_s+s\,p_s}=\frac{p_s}{p_s+(1-p_s)/s} \]

With \(s=0.1\) and \(p_s=0.2\), \(p\approx 0.0244\); multiplying by \(s\) (0.02) is only the small-probability approximation. In serving, add \(\log s\) to the logit.

Alternatives. Weight each kept negative by \(1/s\) in the loss: the weighted loss is unbiased for the full-data loss, so outputs are calibrated directly, at the price of higher gradient variance. Class weights \(w_1, w_0\) in BCE move the optimum to \(w_1 q/(w_1 q + w_0(1-q))\), a logit offset of \(\log(w_1/w_0)\) that you undo the same way.

When. Negative downsampling is a compute decision; it loses little when negatives vastly outnumber positives. Imbalance itself does not break a probabilistic model trained with log loss; what matters is the absolute number of positives. So the default is the natural distribution (or downsample and correct), a threshold chosen from costs, and evaluation on unsampled data. Oversampling duplicates rows and invites overfitting, and focal loss or class weights break calibration unless corrected.

Do not correct twice (weights and offset together), and use per-example rates if sampling depended on features.

  • Hard-negative mining gives each example its own sampling rate. How do you correct then?
  • Which of ROC-AUC, PR-AUC and log loss change when you evaluate on the downsampled set?
corederivationMLE AI REasked at Apple Derive the logistic regression loss from maximum likelihood, then its gradient and Hessian. What do they tell you about optimisation?

Model \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\) as Bernoulli with \(p_i=\sigma(z_i)\), \(z_i=w^\top x_i+b\). The likelihood is \(\prod_i p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}\), so the negative log-likelihood is

\[ L(w)=-\sum_i\big[y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\big]=\sum_i\big[\log(1+e^{z_i})-y_i z_i\big]. \]

Differentiating the second form, \(\partial L/\partial z_i=\sigma(z_i)-y_i=p_i-y_i\), hence

\[ \nabla_w L=X^\top(p-y),\qquad \nabla_w^2 L=X^\top S X,\quad S=\operatorname{diag}\big(p_i(1-p_i)\big). \]

What follows:

  • \(S\succeq 0\), so the loss is convex and any local minimum is global. There is no closed form; use gradient methods, L-BFGS or Newton (IRLS).
  • The gradient is residual times input, the same form as least squares and softmax regression. That is the canonical-link property of GLMs.
  • On linearly separable data there is no finite maximiser: \(\|w\|\to\infty\). Regularise.
  • MSE on a sigmoid output is non-convex in \(w\) and carries a factor \(p(1-p)\) that vanishes exactly when the model is confidently wrong. Log loss cancels that factor.
  • Minimising cross-entropy is MLE and equals minimising \(\mathrm{KL}(\text{data}\,\|\,\text{model})\) up to a constant. It is a proper scoring rule, so a well-specified model is calibrated at the optimum.
  • With labels in \(\{-1,+1\}\) the same loss is \(\log(1+e^{-y z})\).
  • Numerics: compute from logits (np.logaddexp(0, z) - y*z, BCEWithLogitsLoss), never log(sigmoid(z)).

Follow-ups

  • What happens to the weights on perfectly separable data, and what does L2 change?
  • Write the multiclass version and its gradient.

coretrade-offMLE AI RE When do ROC-AUC, PR-AUC and log loss disagree, and which would you report for a classifier with 0.1% positives?

They measure different things.

  • ROC-AUC is \(P(\text{score of a random positive} > \text{score of a random negative})\). It depends only on the ranking, and is invariant to the class prior and to any monotone transform of the scores. With rare positives the false-positive rate has a huge denominator: at 0.1% prevalence a 1% FPR already means about ten false positives per positive, so ROC can look excellent while precision is poor.
  • PR-AUC (average precision) uses precision \(TP/(TP+FP)\), which depends on prevalence. A random classifier scores the prevalence, not 0.5, so values are not comparable across datasets. It concentrates on the top of the ranking.
  • Log loss is a proper scoring rule: it rewards calibration and sharpness, and is hurt by a few confident errors and by prior shift. A monotone rescaling changes log loss but not AUC. Compare it with the entropy of the base rate, \(-\pi\log\pi-(1-\pi)\log(1-\pi)\).

Typical disagreements: better AUC with worse log loss means miscalibration, which is fixable post hoc; better log loss with the same AUC means only calibration improved.

At 0.1% positives I report the operating-point metric the product uses (precision at a fixed recall, recall at \(k\) reviews per day, recall at a fixed FPR), PR-AUC as a summary, and calibration if the scores are consumed as probabilities. All with intervals: with 100 positives in the test set, the standard error of recall can be 5 points.

  • Why does negative downsampling leave ROC-AUC unchanged in expectation but change PR-AUC?
  • Two models have the same AUC. How can one still be much better in production?
coreconceptMLE AI RE Define NDCG@k. Why is it preferred to precision@k for graded relevance, and what are its pitfalls?

\[ \mathrm{DCG@}k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{rel_i}-1}{\log_2(i+1)}, \qquad \mathrm{NDCG@}k=\frac{\mathrm{DCG@}k}{\mathrm{IDCG@}k} \]

IDCG is the DCG of the ideal ordering for that query, so NDCG lies in \([0,1]\) and is averaged over queries. Example: grades \([2,3,0]\) give \(\mathrm{DCG}=3+7/\log_2 3=7.42\); the ideal order \([3,2,0]\) gives \(8.89\); NDCG is \(0.83\).

It uses graded labels and discounts by position. Precision@k is binary and ignores order inside the top \(k\); MRR looks only at the first relevant result; MAP is binary.

Pitfalls:

  • There are two gain conventions, \(2^{rel}-1\) and \(rel\). Say which one.
  • A query with no relevant document has \(\mathrm{IDCG}=0\); scoring it 0 versus skipping it changes the mean.
  • Per-query normalisation weights every query equally, whatever its traffic.
  • Unjudged documents are usually counted as irrelevant, which penalises a new system that surfaces documents the old one never showed.
  • The log discount is a convention, not a measured examination curve; click-derived labels also carry position bias.
  • It is piecewise constant in the scores, so training uses surrogates: pairwise logistic loss, LambdaRank / LambdaMART (pair gradients weighted by the \(|\Delta\mathrm{NDCG}|\) of swapping the pair), or listwise softmax.

Follow-ups

  • How does LambdaMART optimise NDCG if NDCG has zero gradient almost everywhere?
  • What does the unjudged-equals-irrelevant rule do when you compare a new retriever with the incumbent?
corecodingMLE AI REasked at Meta Implement k-means in NumPy with k-means++ initialisation. State the objective, why the algorithm converges, and where it fails. 
import numpy as np

def kmeanspp(X, k, rng):
    C = [X[rng.integers(len(X))]]
    d2 = ((X - C[0]) ** 2).sum(1)                     # squared distance to the nearest centre so far
    for _ in range(k - 1):
        C.append(X[rng.choice(len(X), p=d2 / d2.sum())])   # sample proportional to D^2
        d2 = np.minimum(d2, ((X - C[-1]) ** 2).sum(1))
    return np.array(C)

def kmeans(X, k, iters=100, tol=1e-8, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    C = kmeanspp(X, k, rng)
    for _ in range(iters):
        d2 = (X**2).sum(1)[:, None] - 2 * X @ C.T + (C**2).sum(1)   # (n, k), no (n, k, d) tensor
        a = d2.argmin(1)                              # assignment step
        newC = C.copy()
        for j in range(k):
            if (a == j).any():                        # empty cluster: keep the old centre
                newC[j] = X[a == j].mean(0)           # update step
        shift = np.abs(newC - C).max()
        C = newC
        if shift < tol:
            break
    return C, a

Objective: \(J=\sum_i\|x_i-c_{a_i}\|^2\). The assignment step minimises \(J\) over assignments with centres fixed; the update step minimises it over centres with assignments fixed (the mean minimises squared distance). \(J\) never increases and there are finitely many partitions, so it converges, but only to a local optimum. Each iteration costs \(O(nkd)\).

Pitfalls:

  • Initialisation matters. Random initialisation can put two centres in one true cluster; k-means++ gives an \(O(\log k)\) approximation in expectation, and you still run several restarts and keep the lowest \(J\).
  • Empty clusters must be handled.
  • Features must be scaled, because Euclidean distance is scale dependent.
  • It assumes roughly spherical clusters of similar size. It is the hard-assignment limit of EM for a Gaussian mixture with shared spherical covariance.
  • \(k\) is chosen from outside the algorithm (elbow, silhouette, or the downstream task); \(J\) always falls as \(k\) grows.

Follow-ups

  • Why does the mean minimise the within-cluster sum of squares, and what would you use for L1 distance?
  • How would you make this work for 100 million points?

seniorcodingMLE AI REasked at TikTok Implement ROC-AUC from scratch in O(n log n), handling tied scores correctly. Explain why the formula works. 
import numpy as np

def roc_auc(y, s):
    y, s = np.asarray(y), np.asarray(s)
    _, inv, cnt = np.unique(s, return_inverse=True, return_counts=True)
    last = np.cumsum(cnt)                   # 1-based rank of the last item in each tie group
    ranks = (last - (cnt - 1) / 2)[inv]     # average rank within ties
    n_pos = y.sum()
    n_neg = len(y) - n_pos
    return (ranks[y == 1].sum() - n_pos * (n_pos + 1) / 2) / (n_pos * n_neg)

Why it works. AUC is the fraction of (positive, negative) pairs ranked correctly, with ties counting one half; this is the Mann-Whitney U statistic divided by \(n_+ n_-\). A positive with ascending rank \(r\) has \(r-1\) items below it. Summing over positives counts positive-negative pairs plus the \(n_+(n_+-1)/2\) positive-positive pairs; subtracting those leaves \(U=\sum_{i\in +} r_i - n_+(n_++1)/2\). Average ranks give tied pairs the half credit.

What an interviewer checks:

  • Ties. Sorting and ranking without tie handling makes the result depend on sort order. A constant score must give exactly 0.5.
  • Complexity: the sort dominates, \(O(n\log n)\). The brute-force pair count is \(O(n_+ n_-)\) and is a good test oracle.
  • Edge case: with only one class present, AUC is undefined. Raise or return NaN, not 0.
  • Equivalent route: sweep thresholds over sorted scores, accumulate TPR and FPR, integrate with the trapezoid rule, grouping tied scores into one step.
  • Per-group AUC (per query or per user) is a different metric from global AUC and is often the one a ranking product needs.

Follow-ups

  • How would you compute a confidence interval for this AUC?
  • Modify it to compute average precision. What changes about tie handling?

coreconceptMLE AI RE An A/B test reports p = 0.03 and a 95% confidence interval of [0.1%, 1.9%] for the lift. State precisely what each number means, and name the most common misreadings.

p-value: the probability, under the null hypothesis and the test’s model assumptions, of a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. It is \(P(\text{data this extreme}\mid H_0)\), not \(P(H_0\mid\text{data})\).

95% confidence interval: the output of a procedure that covers the true parameter in 95% of repeated experiments. This particular interval either contains it or does not. “95% probability the lift is in the interval” is a Bayesian credible-interval statement and needs a prior. The two are linked: a 95% interval that excludes zero corresponds to a two-sided \(p<0.05\) from the same test.

Misreadings:

  1. Reading \(p\) as the chance the null is true. That depends on the prior and the power: if 10% of tested ideas are real, with 80% power and \(\alpha=0.05\), the share of significant results that are false is \(0.045/(0.045+0.08)=36\%\).
  2. Not significant is not the same as no effect. Look at the interval width.
  3. Statistical versus practical significance. With millions of users, negligible effects reach significance.
  4. Significant estimates from under-powered tests overstate the effect (winner’s curse).
  5. The guarantee assumes a fixed sample size and one pre-chosen metric. Peeking and many metrics break it.
  6. The analysis unit must match the randomisation unit. Randomising users but analysing page views understates the variance; use the delta method or a cluster bootstrap.

Report the effect size with its interval, not only the p-value.

import random

def reservoir_sample(stream, k, rng=random):
    res = []
    for i, x in enumerate(stream):      # i is 0-based; i + 1 items seen so far
        if i < k:
            res.append(x)
        else:
            j = rng.randint(0, i)       # inclusive on both ends: uniform over i + 1 values
            if j < k:
                res[j] = x              # happens with probability k / (i + 1)
    return res

Proof by induction. Claim: after \(m\ge k\) items, each is in the reservoir with probability \(k/m\). It holds at \(m=k\). When item \(m+1\) arrives it enters with probability \(k/(m+1)\). An item already in the reservoir survives unless the newcomer enters and picks its slot: \(1-\frac{k}{m+1}\cdot\frac1k=\frac{m}{m+1}\). So its probability becomes \(\frac{k}{m}\cdot\frac{m}{m+1}=\frac{k}{m+1}\).

Time is \(O(n)\), memory \(O(k)\).

What an interviewer checks:

  • Off-by-one in the random index. randint(0, i) is inclusive; with NumPy use rng.integers(0, i + 1).

  • Weighted version (Efraimidis-Spirakis): give each item the key \(u^{1/w}\) with \(u\sim U(0,1)\) and keep the \(k\) largest keys in a heap.
  • Distributed version: reservoirs from shards cannot simply be concatenated; merge by sampling from each in proportion to its shard’s count, or use the random-key method (keep the \(k\) smallest uniform keys), which merges trivially.
  • Typical uses: an unbiased sample of logs for labelling or evaluation, and bounded replay buffers.

seniorconceptMLE AI REasked at Apple Two annotators agree on 85% of items. Is that good? Explain how you measure label quality and what label noise does to training and to evaluation.

It cannot be judged without chance agreement. Cohen’s kappa corrects for it:

\[ \kappa=\frac{p_o-p_e}{1-p_e},\qquad p_e=\sum_c p_{1,c}\,p_{2,c} \]

where \(p_{a,c}\) is the share of items annotator \(a\) assigns to class \(c\). With balanced binary labels \(p_e=0.5\) and \(\kappa=0.70\). If both annotators mark 90% negative, \(p_e=0.82\) and \(\kappa\approx0.17\): barely above chance. For more than two annotators use Fleiss’ kappa; for missing ratings or ordinal scales, Krippendorff’s alpha. Kappa moves with prevalence, so report per-class agreement and the annotator confusion matrix as well.

What low agreement means. Unclear guidelines (the cheapest thing to fix: tighten the guideline and add examples), genuinely ambiguous items (keep the label distribution instead of forcing a majority), or weak annotators (gold questions, per-annotator accuracy; Dawid-Skene estimates each annotator’s confusion matrix and the true labels jointly with EM).

import numpy as np

def sigmoid(z):
    return np.exp(-np.logaddexp(0, -z))          # stable for large |z|

def fit_logreg(X, y, lr=0.1, l2=1e-4, epochs=20, batch=64, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n, d = X.shape
    w, b = np.zeros(d), 0.0
    for _ in range(epochs):
        perm = rng.permutation(n)                 # reshuffle every epoch
        for i in range(0, n, batch):
            idx = perm[i:i + batch]
            g = sigmoid(X[idx] @ w + b) - y[idx]  # dL/dz = p - y
            w -= lr * (X[idx].T @ g / len(idx) + l2 * w)
            b -= lr * g.mean()                    # no penalty on the bias
    return w, b

def log_loss(X, y, w, b):
    z = X @ w + b
    return np.mean(np.logaddexp(0, z) - y * z)    # log(1 + e^z) - y z

seniorderivationMLE REasked at TikTok Derive how XGBoost sets a leaf weight and scores a candidate split. Why second order, and what do lambda, gamma and min_child_weight do?

Boosting adds one tree per round: \(\hat y^{(t)}=\hat y^{(t-1)}+f_t(x)\). Expand the loss to second order around the current prediction, with \(g_i=\partial_{\hat y}\ell(y_i,\hat y_i^{(t-1)})\) and \(h_i=\partial^2_{\hat y}\ell\):

\[ \text{obj}^{(t)}\approx\sum_i\Big[g_if_t(x_i)+\tfrac12h_if_t(x_i)^2\Big]+\gamma T+\tfrac12\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{T}w_j^2 \]

A tree sends each example to one of \(T\) leaves with weight \(w_j\). With \(G_j=\sum_{i\in j}g_i\) and \(H_j=\sum_{i\in j}h_i\) the objective separates by leaf, \(\sum_j\big[G_jw_j+\tfrac12(H_j+\lambda)w_j^2\big]+\gamma T\), so

\[ w_j^*=-\frac{G_j}{H_j+\lambda},\qquad \text{gain}=\frac12\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda}+\frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda}-\frac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda}\right]-\gamma . \]

Split search. For each feature, scan sorted values (exact) or at most a few hundred histogram bins (hist, LightGBM), accumulating \(G_L,H_L\), and keep the best gain. Missing values get a learned default direction by trying both sides.

Why second order. The leaf weight is a Newton step, so each leaf gets a correctly scaled step for any twice-differentiable loss. For squared loss \(h=1\) and it reduces to the mean residual. For log loss \(g=p-y\) and \(h=p(1-p)\).

Regularisers. \(\lambda\) shrinks leaf weights, most where \(H\) is small. \(\gamma\) is the minimum gain needed to keep a split. min_child_weight is a lower bound on a child’s \(H\); for log loss \(H\) is small where predictions are already confident, so it stops splitting nearly pure nodes. Shrinkage (the learning rate) and row or column subsampling act on top.

Variants: LightGBM grows leaf-wise and adds GOSS and feature bundling; CatBoost uses ordered boosting, ordered target statistics and symmetric trees.

import numpy as np

def best_split(X, y):
    """Return (gain, feature, threshold) maximising the reduction in squared error."""
    n, d = X.shape
    total = y.sum()
    best = (0.0, None, None)
    for j in range(d):
        order = np.argsort(X[:, j], kind="mergesort")
        xs, ys = X[order, j], y[order]
        left = np.cumsum(ys)[:-1]                 # sum of y in the left child for a split after row i
        nl = np.arange(1, n)
        gain = left**2 / nl + (total - left)**2 / (n - nl) - total**2 / n
        gain[xs[1:] == xs[:-1]] = -np.inf         # cannot split between equal values
        i = int(gain.argmax())
        if gain[i] > best[0]:
            best = (float(gain[i]), j, float((xs[i] + xs[i + 1]) / 2))
    return best

Why the formula. The squared error of a node around its mean is \(\sum y^2-S^2/n\) with \(S=\sum y\). The \(\sum y^2\) terms cancel between parent and children, so

One sort and one prefix sum per feature evaluate every threshold in \(O(n)\) after an \(O(n\log n)\) sort. Recomputing child means per threshold is the \(O(n^2)\) version.

seniortrade-offMLE AI RE Why do gradient-boosted trees still tend to beat neural networks on tabular data, and when would you pick a linear model or a neural network instead?

Tabular data has heterogeneous features, targets with sharp thresholds, many uninformative columns and usually \(10^4\) to \(10^6\) rows. The inductive bias of trees fits that; an MLP’s does not.

  • Splits are axis-aligned and invariant to monotone transforms: no scaling, heavy tails are harmless, missing values are handled natively.
  • Piecewise-constant functions capture thresholds and interactions directly, while MLPs are biased towards smooth functions.
  • Greedy split selection ignores useless features. An MLP is rotation invariant and needs more data to find which axes matter (Grinsztajn et al., 2022, on medium-sized benchmarks).
  • Good defaults, minutes on CPU, little tuning.

Boosting versus random forest. Boosting reduces bias with many shallow trees fit sequentially; it needs a learning rate and early stopping and is more sensitive to label noise. A forest averages deep decorrelated trees to cut variance; it parallelises, is hard to mis-tune and gives out-of-bag estimates.

Where trees lose. Extrapolation: predictions are constant outside the training range, which hurts with trends. Very high-cardinality ids, raw text, images and sequences. Online updates. Multi-task learning and shared representations.

Pick a linear model for very sparse high-dimensional features with huge data and online updates (hashed crosses with FTRL), when monotonic, auditable scores are required (credit), for microsecond latency, and always as the baseline.

Pick a neural network when the signal is in ids and sequences (large recommender systems), when inputs are multimodal, or when pretraining transfers. Hybrids are common: embeddings fed to a GBDT, or GBDT leaf indices fed to a linear model.

Tabular foundation models report wins on small datasets; treat that as benchmark specific and always run a tuned GBDT baseline.

seniordebuggingMLE You add a target encoding of merchant id and validation AUC jumps, but the model does worse online. What happened, and how do you do target encoding safely?

The encoding of each training row included that row’s own label. For a rare category the encoding is almost the label itself (a merchant with one row is encoded as its \(y\)), so the model learns to trust the feature. At serving time the encoding comes from other rows and is far noisier. If the encoding was fit before the split, validation rows leaked in the same way, which is why validation looked good.

Doing it safely:

  1. Out-of-fold. Encode each training row from the other folds only; encode validation and test rows from training data only. Fit the encoder inside each CV fold.
  2. Smooth towards the prior. \(\hat\mu_c=\dfrac{n_c\,\bar y_c+m\,\bar y}{n_c+m}\), where \(m\) is a pseudo-count. Rare categories shrink to the global mean. This is the posterior mean of a Beta-Binomial model.
  3. Respect time. Use only labels that existed, and had matured, before the row’s timestamp. CatBoost’s ordered target statistics apply the same idea over random permutations. This is also what the online feature can actually see.
  4. Unseen categories get the prior. Add the count \(n_c\) as a feature so the model knows how much to trust the encoding.

How to recognise the leak: feature importance dominated by the encoded column; the encoded values are more extreme on training rows than on validation rows; performance collapses on low-count categories.

Label-free alternatives are count or frequency encoding, hashing with a linear model, and learned embeddings.

coreconceptMLE RE How are PCA, the eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix and the SVD related? What does each give you computationally, and where does PCA mislead?

Centre \(X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\). The covariance \(C=\frac{1}{n-1}X^\top X=V\Lambda V^\top\) is symmetric positive semi-definite, so it has orthonormal eigenvectors and non-negative eigenvalues. The SVD \(X=U\Sigma V^\top\) has the same \(V\), with \(\lambda_i=s_i^2/(n-1)\) for singular values \(s_i\). The principal directions are the columns of \(V\), the projected data is \(XV=U\Sigma\), and component \(i\) explains \(s_i^2/\sum_j s_j^2\) of the variance.

Optimality (Eckart-Young). The truncated SVD \(X_k=U_k\Sigma_kV_k^\top\) is the best rank-\(k\) approximation in Frobenius and spectral norm, with Frobenius error \(\sqrt{\sum_{i>k}s_i^2}\). Maximising projected variance and minimising reconstruction error are the same problem, and a linear autoencoder recovers the same subspace.

Computation. Take the SVD of \(X\) rather than the eigendecomposition of \(X^\top X\): forming the product squares the condition number. Full SVD costs \(O(\min(nd^2,n^2d))\); for the top \(k\) use randomised SVD or Lanczos, about \(O(ndk)\).

Eigen versus SVD. Eigendecomposition needs a square matrix, and orthogonal eigenvectors need a symmetric (normal) one. The SVD exists for every matrix. For symmetric PSD matrices the two coincide.

Where PCA misleads. It is scale dependent, so standardise. High variance is not relevance to the label; the discriminative direction can be a low-variance one. It is linear, sensitive to outliers, and its components are defined only up to sign (and up to rotation when eigenvalues tie). Fit it on training data only.

The same decomposition gives the pseudo-inverse \(X^{+}=V\Sigma^{+}U^\top\) (minimum-norm least squares), the condition number \(s_{\max}/s_{\min}\), and the low-rank view behind LoRA and embedding compression.

seniorconceptMLE RE What makes an optimisation problem convex, how do you check it from the Hessian, and what do the Hessian’s eigenvalues tell you about how gradient descent behaves?

\(f\) is convex if its domain is convex and \(f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\le\theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y)\). Equivalently, the function lies above its tangents, or, for twice-differentiable \(f\), \(\nabla^2f\succeq0\) everywhere. Then every local minimum is global; with strong convexity (\(\nabla^2f\succeq\mu I\), \(\mu>0\)) the minimiser is unique.

Convex: least squares (Hessian \(X^\top X\)), logistic loss (\(X^\top SX\)), hinge loss, L1 and L2 penalties, log-sum-exp, sums and maxima of convex functions. Not convex: anything with a hidden layer (permuting hidden units already gives equivalent minima), matrix factorisation \(\|M-UV^\top\|^2\) (convex in each factor separately, hence alternating least squares), k-means.

Gradient descent on a quadratic with Hessian eigenvalues in \([\mu,L]\):

  • It converges only if \(\eta<2/L\): the largest eigenvalue caps the learning rate.
  • With \(\eta=1/L\) the error along an eigen-direction with eigenvalue \(h\) shrinks by \(1-h/L\) per step, so the flattest direction contracts by \(1-1/\kappa\), where \(\kappa=L/\mu\) is the condition number. \(\kappa=1000\) means thousands of steps.
  • Remedies all precondition: standardising features, momentum (dependence on \(\sqrt\kappa\) instead of \(\kappa\)), Newton’s method (rescales by \(H^{-1}\) at \(O(d^3)\) cost), Adam-style diagonal scaling, normalisation layers.

Deep networks. In high dimension, saddle points are far more common than poor local minima, and gradient noise helps leave them. With full-batch gradient descent the top Hessian eigenvalue is observed to rise until it hovers near \(2/\eta\) (the edge of stability; Cohen et al., 2021).

Mechanics. The gradient of a scalar is a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\); the Hessian is \(d\times d\) and symmetric. You almost never form it: a Hessian-vector product costs about two backward passes.

Maximise \(H(p)=-\sum_k p_k\log p_k\) subject to \(\sum_k p_k=1\) and \(\sum_k p_ks_k=c\).

\[ \mathcal{L}=-\sum_k p_k\log p_k+\lambda\Big(\sum_k p_ks_k-c\Big)+\nu\Big(\sum_k p_k-1\Big) \]

Stationarity in \(p_k\): \(-\log p_k-1+\lambda s_k+\nu=0\), so \(p_k\propto e^{\lambda s_k}\), and normalising gives

\[ p_k=\frac{e^{\lambda s_k}}{\sum_j e^{\lambda s_j}}. \]

The multiplier \(\lambda\) is fixed by the constraint value \(c\) and acts as an inverse temperature. The objective is concave and the constraints are linear, so this stationary point is the global maximum; the solution is strictly positive, so the constraints \(p_k\ge0\) are inactive.

KKT conditions for \(\min f(x)\) subject to \(g_i(x)\le0\), \(h_j(x)=0\), with \(\mathcal{L}=f+\sum_i\mu_ig_i+\sum_j\nu_jh_j\):

  1. Stationarity: \(\nabla_x\mathcal{L}=0\).
  2. Primal feasibility: \(g_i\le0\), \(h_j=0\).
  3. Dual feasibility: \(\mu_i\ge0\).
  4. Complementary slackness: \(\mu_ig_i(x)=0\).

For a convex problem satisfying Slater’s condition they are necessary and sufficient, and strong duality holds.

  • SVM: complementary slackness gives non-zero multipliers only for points on or inside the margin (the support vectors), and the dual touches the data only through inner products, which is what admits kernels.
  • Ridge and lasso: the penalised form and the constrained form \(\|w\|\le t\) are the same problem, with \(\lambda\) the multiplier.
  • A multiplier is a price: the rate of change of the optimum per unit of constraint relaxation. Budget pacing, constrained RL and “maximise engagement subject to a guardrail” are solved by ascending on \(\lambda\) while optimising the Lagrangian.

seniorconceptMLE RE Give a Bayesian treatment of estimating a click-through rate from a handful of impressions. How does it relate to MLE, MAP and regularisation, and when is the extra machinery worth it?

Prior \(\theta\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\); data \(k\) clicks in \(n\) impressions. The Beta is conjugate to the binomial, so the posterior is \(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha+k,\ \beta+n-k)\) with mean

\[ \frac{\alpha+k}{\alpha+\beta+n}=\frac{n}{n+\alpha+\beta}\cdot\frac{k}{n}+\frac{\alpha+\beta}{n+\alpha+\beta}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}. \]

The prior acts as \(\alpha+\beta\) pseudo-impressions. With 0 clicks in 3 impressions the MLE says 0; under \(\mathrm{Beta}(1,19)\) (prior mean 5%) the posterior mean is \(1/23\approx4.3\%\). The posterior mean is also the predictive probability of a click on the next impression.

Relations. MLE maximises \(p(D\mid\theta)\). MAP maximises \(p(D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)\), which is MLE plus a regulariser: a Gaussian prior gives L2 and a Laplace prior gives L1. MAP is still a point estimate. Full Bayes keeps the posterior and predicts with \(p(y^*\mid D)=\int p(y^*\mid\theta)\,p(\theta\mid D)\,d\theta\).

What the posterior buys.

  • Uncertainty that shrinks with data, hence credible intervals.
  • Exploration: Thompson sampling draws \(\theta\) from the posterior and acts greedily on the draw.
  • Partial pooling: estimate the prior from all items (empirical Bayes, hierarchical models) so long-tail items borrow strength. Smoothed target encoding is this.

Vocabulary. Epistemic uncertainty comes from limited data and is reducible; aleatoric is noise in the outcome and is not.

Beyond conjugacy. MCMC (asymptotically exact, slow), variational inference (maximise an ELBO; fast, tends to under-estimate variance), Laplace approximation. Gaussian processes give an exact posterior over functions at \(O(n^3)\) cost and are used for small data and Bayesian optimisation. For deep networks, ensembles and MC dropout are the pragmatic approximations, and ensembles are usually the stronger baseline.

It is worth it with little data per entity, a need for exploration, or decisions that depend on uncertainty. With abundant data the posterior concentrates and a point estimate is enough.

seniorconceptMLE RE Users who received a push notification show 30% higher retention. Why can’t you read that as the effect of the notification, and how would you estimate the effect from observational data?

Notifications were targeted, so treated and untreated users differ in ways that also drive retention. That is confounding:

\[ \mathbb{E}[Y\mid T{=}1]-\mathbb{E}[Y\mid T{=}0] =\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid T{=}1]}_{\text{effect on the treated}} +\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y(0)\mid T{=}1]-\mathbb{E}[Y(0)\mid T{=}0]}_{\text{selection bias}} \]

Randomising removes the second term. Without it, adjustment needs three assumptions: unconfoundedness \((Y(0),Y(1))\perp T\mid X\), which is untestable; overlap \(0<e(x)<1\) for the propensity \(e(x)=P(T{=}1\mid X{=}x)\); and no interference between units (SUTVA).

Estimators:

  • Regression adjustment: model \(\mu_t(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X{=}x,T{=}t]\) and average \(\mu_1-\mu_0\).
  • Inverse propensity weighting: \[ \hat\tau_{\text{IPW}}=\frac1n\sum_i\left[\frac{T_iY_i}{\hat e(X_i)}-\frac{(1-T_i)Y_i}{1-\hat e(X_i)}\right] \]
  • Doubly robust (AIPW): the regression estimate plus an IPW correction of its residuals. It is consistent if either the outcome model or the propensity model is correct.

Failure modes:

  • Propensities near 0 or 1 give huge weights. Clip or trim, use self-normalised weights, and report the effective sample size. A propensity model with AUC near 1 means there is no overlap, not that the model is good.
  • Adjust only for pre-treatment covariates. Conditioning on a mediator or a collider adds bias.
  • Unmeasured confounders: run sensitivity analysis and negative controls, or look for an instrument or natural experiment.

Checks: covariate balance after weighting (standardised mean differences below about 0.1) and a placebo outcome, for example retention before the notification, which should show no effect.

The same weighting underlies off-policy evaluation and position-bias correction in ranking.

stafftrade-offMLE Two causal questions. (a) A feature launched in one country only, with no experiment. How do you estimate its effect? (b) You ran a randomised promotion experiment and must decide who gets the promotion. How is that different from predicting who will buy?

(a) Difference-in-differences.

\[ \hat\tau=(\bar Y_{T,\text{post}}-\bar Y_{T,\text{pre}})-(\bar Y_{C,\text{post}}-\bar Y_{C,\text{pre}}) \]

It removes fixed differences between the groups and shocks common to both. The assumption is parallel trends: without the launch, both would have moved alike. It cannot be tested after launch; probe it with pre-period trends (an event-study plot whose pre-launch coefficients should be near zero) and placebo launch dates. Threats are concurrent events in one group, spillovers, anticipation and a changing user mix. With one treated country there are very few independent clusters, so naive standard errors are far too small; use cluster-robust or permutation inference. If no control is parallel, a synthetic control builds a weighted mix of untreated units that tracks the pre-period.

(b) Uplift. The target is \(\tau(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X{=}x]\), not \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X{=}x]\). Likely buyers include “sure things” who buy anyway, so targeting on purchase propensity wastes the budget; you want those whose behaviour changes. Nobody is observed under both arms, so there is no per-row label.

  • Methods: T-learner (one model per arm, subtract; noisy when the effect is small relative to outcome variance), S-learner (treatment as a feature; tends to shrink the effect towards zero), X-, R- and DR-learners, causal forests. With randomised data and propensity \(e\), the transformed outcome \(Y(T-e)/(e(1-e))\) has conditional mean \(\tau(x)\).
  • Evaluation: rank by predicted uplift and compare treated against control outcomes within each top fraction (uplift or Qini curve), then A/B test the targeting policy against treating everyone and against random targeting.
  • Decision: treat when \(\tau(x)\times\text{value}>\text{cost}\); under a budget, rank by uplift per unit cost.

Effects are small relative to noise, so uplift models need much larger experiments than response models.
