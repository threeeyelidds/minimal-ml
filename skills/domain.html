The senior bar

What separates a senior answer from a mid-level one in this area.

  • States what is different about the domain before proposing a model - where labels come from and what they cost, how the data is biased, what a failure costs, and which constraints (latency, power, regulation, safety) are hard.
  • Knows the standard problem formulations and metrics of the domain (mAP, WER, minADE, closed-loop success, PPV at prevalence, deflated Sharpe) and what each metric hides.
  • Chooses evaluation that matches deployment - closed loop for systems that act, external sites for clinical models, time-ordered and purged splits for finance, family or time splits for security, prospective experiments for science.
  • Reasons about physical and real-time constraints with numbers - sensor rates, control frequencies, latency budgets, compute power envelopes.
  • Separates what is established from what is contested or changing fast (end-to-end driving, vision-language-action models, generative world models) and says so plainly.
  • Transfers general ML judgment honestly, naming which parts of web-scale practice carry over (data engines, experiment discipline, monitoring) and which do not (cheap labels, A/B tests, tolerance for errors).

coretrade-offMLE RE Imitation learning or reinforcement learning for a robot skill? Explain the trade-off and when each is used.

Behaviour cloning treats control as supervised learning from demonstrations, \(\min_\theta\mathbb{E}_{(o,a)\sim D}\,\ell(\pi_\theta(o),a)\).

  • For: no reward design, no unsafe exploration, stable training, works directly from camera images.
  • Against: compounding error. The policy’s small mistakes take it to states the demonstrator never visited; with per-step error \(\epsilon\) the cost can grow as \(O(\epsilon T^2)\) over horizon \(T\), versus \(O(\epsilon T)\) when corrective data is collected interactively (DAgger). Performance is capped by the demonstrations, and human demonstrations are multi-modal (several valid ways to do a task), which a regression loss averages into an invalid action; hence generative action heads (diffusion or flow policies, discretised tokens).

Reinforcement learning optimises expected return through trial and error.

  • For: can exceed the demonstrator, discovers recovery behaviour, needs no demonstrations.
  • Against: sample-inefficient, needs a reward and resets, exploration on hardware is slow and risky. So it is mostly done in simulation, which brings the sim-to-real gap.

Where each wins today.

  • Legged locomotion: RL in massively parallel simulation with domain randomisation, transferred zero-shot, is the standard. Dynamics are simulated well enough and the reward (track a velocity, stay upright, save energy) is easy to write.
  • Manipulation: imitation from teleoperated demonstrations dominates, because contact and deformable objects are hard to simulate and rewards are hard to specify. Large vision-language-action models are trained this way.
  • Combinations: pretrain by imitation, then fine-tune with RL in the real world or in simulation for robustness and speed; use RL with a learned or sparse success reward to go beyond demonstration quality.

Different from product ML: data is collected by physical robots at human cost, evaluation means running real trials with high variance, and the model acts in a closed loop at tens of hertz.

  • How does DAgger work and why is it awkward with human teleoperators?
  • Why does mean-squared-error regression fail on multi-modal demonstrations?
seniorconceptMLE RE What is a vision-language-action model? Describe the architecture, how actions are represented, where the data comes from, and the open problems.

A VLA is a policy built on a pretrained vision-language model: it takes camera images, a language instruction and usually proprioception, and outputs robot actions. The bet is that web-scale pretraining supplies semantic and visual generalisation that robot data alone is too small to give.

Action representation, the main design axis:

  • Discrete tokens: discretise each action dimension into bins (256 in RT-2 and OpenVLA) and emit them as tokens from the language model’s vocabulary. Simple and reuses the training stack, but slow (one token per dimension per step) and coarse for high-rate dexterous control. Compressed tokenisations of whole action chunks reduce the token count.
  • Continuous generative heads: a diffusion or flow-matching “action expert” attached to the VLM backbone generates a chunk of future actions in a few denoising steps. Handles multi-modal, high-frequency actions.
  • Two-rate systems: a large VLM runs at a few hertz to produce a latent plan or subgoal, and a small fast policy runs at tens to hundreds of hertz conditioned on it.

Data.

  • Teleoperated demonstrations on the target robots: highest value, most expensive.
  • Cross-embodiment datasets pooling many labs and robots (Open X-Embodiment is on the order of a million trajectories), which is tiny next to web text and image data.
  • Co-training with web vision-language data to keep semantic knowledge.
  • Simulation and human video, each with a gap to close (physics and embodiment respectively).

Open problems.

  • Evaluation: real-robot success rates over tens of trials have wide confidence intervals and depend on the setup; simulation benchmarks need their correlation with real results checked.
  • Generalisation: good transfer to new objects and instructions, weaker to new motions, new embodiments and long-horizon tasks.
  • Latency: a multi-billion-parameter model cannot run inside a fast control loop without chunking and asynchronous execution.
  • Reliability: success rates acceptable for a demo are far from what unattended deployment needs.

This area moves quickly; treat specific model claims as version-dependent.

Follow-ups

  • Why not just fine-tune a VLM to output actions as text?
  • How would you evaluate two policies fairly with fifty real trials each?
seniorconceptMLE RE What is action chunking and why does it help imitation policies? Then explain the real-time problem it creates when the policy is slower than the control loop.

Action chunking: the policy predicts a sequence of the next \(k\) actions from the current observation and executes some or all of them open loop before predicting again, instead of one action per step.

Why it helps:

  • Shorter effective horizon. Fewer decision points per task means fewer opportunities for compounding error.
  • Temporal consistency. Human demonstrations contain pauses and multi-modal choices that are not explained by the current observation; a single-step policy can dither between modes or freeze at a pause. Committing to a chunk keeps one mode.
  • Throughput. One inference call yields many actions, which is what makes a slow model usable.

The cost is reactivity: during a chunk the robot does not respond to new observations. Typical choices: predict a longer horizon than is executed (for example predict 16 steps, execute 8), or re-predict every step and average overlapping predictions with exponential weights (temporal ensembling), which is smooth but can blur distinct modes.

The real-time problem. Layers run at different rates: a learned policy at roughly 1-50 Hz, a low-level joint controller at hundreds of hertz to 1 kHz. If inference takes \(\delta\) seconds, then by the time a chunk arrives the robot has moved for \(\delta\), and the first \(\delta\times f\) actions of the new chunk are stale. Naively switching chunks causes discontinuities and jerky motion.

Remedies:

  • Asynchronous inference: keep executing the current chunk while the next is computed, and start the new chunk at the action index matching the present time.
  • Consistency across chunks: condition the new chunk on the actions already committed (treat them as fixed and generate the rest), so the trajectory is continuous.
  • Fewer denoising steps or distilled samplers for diffusion and flow policies; quantisation and compiled inference on the onboard computer.
  • A fast reactive layer below the policy for contact and safety (impedance control, limits, watchdogs), which does not wait for the network.

Report the control rate, the inference latency and the executed horizon together; a policy evaluated with zero latency in simulation can fail on hardware for this reason alone.

Follow-ups

  • How do you choose the chunk length for a task with fast contact events?
  • What happens to a chunked policy when the camera feed lags by 100 ms?
seniortrade-offMLE RE A policy trained in simulation fails on the real robot. What are the sources of the sim-to-real gap and the standard ways to close it? Where does teleoperation data fit?

Sources of the gap.

  • Dynamics: friction, contact, compliance, actuator delay and saturation, backlash, payload. Rigid-body simulators approximate contact; deformable objects and fluids are worse.
  • Perception: rendered images differ from camera images in texture, lighting, noise and lens effects; depth sensors have characteristic artefacts.
  • Timing: real systems have latency and jitter in sensing and actuation that the simulator steps do not.
  • Unmodelled state: cables, wear, temperature.

Closing it.

  • Domain randomisation: train over wide ranges of physical and visual parameters so the real world looks like one more sample. Too little fails to cover reality; too much yields an over-conservative policy.
  • System identification: measure the real robot’s parameters and fit the simulator; learn actuator models from real data.
  • Privileged teacher, deployable student: train a teacher in simulation with access to true state, then distil into a student that uses only onboard sensors and history, which implicitly estimates the hidden parameters.
  • Adaptation: condition the policy on a latent estimated online from recent observations; fine-tune on a small amount of real data.
  • Abstraction for perception: feed depth, segmentation or keypoints rather than raw RGB, or reconstruct real scenes into the simulator.

Locomotion transfers well because the reward and dynamics are tractable. Contact-rich manipulation with vision transfers poorly, which is why manipulation leans on real data.

Teleoperation data is the main real-data source: an operator drives the robot through a leader arm, VR controller or exoskeleton. Issues that decide its value:

  • Cost and scale: hours of robot and operator time per hour of data.
  • Quality: operator skill varies; slow, hesitant or failed demonstrations teach those behaviours, so episodes are filtered and scored.
  • Diversity over volume: many scenes, objects and starting conditions matter more than repetitions of one setup.
  • Coverage of recovery: demonstrations show success; collecting corrections when the policy fails (intervention data) is what fixes compounding error.

Follow-ups

  • How do you choose randomisation ranges without access to the real robot’s parameters?
  • Would you rather have 1,000 demonstrations in one kitchen or 100 in each of ten?

seniordebuggingMLE AI A diagnostic imaging model validated at AUC 0.93 at the hospital where it was built drops to 0.80 at a second hospital. How do you investigate?

Expect this. Clinical data differs across sites in ways a single-site validation cannot reveal, so an external validation is the minimum bar.

  1. Acquisition shift. Scanner vendor and model, acquisition protocol, resolution, compression, and preprocessing differ. Stratify performance by device and protocol; check that the input pipeline (intensity windowing, orientation, resampling) is identical.
  2. Population and prevalence shift. Different age mix, disease severity, referral pattern (screening versus symptomatic patients). AUC changes when case mix changes even if the model is unchanged: easy, advanced cases at a tertiary centre inflate it. Compare on matched subgroups.
  3. Label shift. How was ground truth defined at each site? Labels extracted from radiology reports, billing codes, a single reader, a consensus panel, or biopsy differ in noise and in meaning. Have a sample from both sites labelled under one protocol with adjudication.
  4. Shortcut learning. The model may have learned site-specific cues correlated with the label at the first hospital: burned-in text, laterality markers, portable-scanner appearance (sicker patients), or devices such as chest drains that indicate an already treated condition. Tests: train a classifier to predict the site or scanner from the model’s features; mask suspected regions; evaluate on the subgroup without the confounder (for example untreated cases only).
  5. Calibration and threshold. Even with preserved ranking, the operating threshold tuned at site one gives different sensitivity and specificity at site two. Recalibrate on local data.

Remedies. Train on multi-site data, with augmentation for acquisition variation; harmonise preprocessing; remove or mask confounders; fine-tune and recalibrate on a small local set; run in silent mode at each new site before going live; monitor input statistics and outcome-linked performance after deployment.

Report sensitivity at a fixed specificity with confidence intervals, per site and per subgroup (sex, age, ethnicity where available, device), not a single pooled AUC.

Follow-ups

  • How would you show that the model uses a shortcut rather than pathology?
  • Why can AUC fall with no change in the model’s per-patient behaviour?
coretrade-offMLE AI What constraints shape deploying an ML model in clinical care that do not exist for a consumer product? Include the arithmetic on predictive value.

Base rates decide usefulness. For a screening model with sensitivity 90%, specificity 95% and disease prevalence 1%:

\[\text{PPV}=\frac{0.9\times0.01}{0.9\times0.01+0.05\times0.99}=\frac{0.009}{0.0585}\approx15\%.\]

Roughly six of seven alerts are false. The same model at 20% prevalence in a referral clinic has a PPV of about 82%. So the intended-use population must be fixed before thresholds are chosen, and evaluation is stated as sensitivity and specificity at that operating point, not AUC alone.

Human oversight is part of the system. Most clinical models advise a clinician. Two failure modes: alert fatigue, where frequent false alarms are overridden reflexively, and automation bias, where clinicians defer to the model when it is wrong. Evaluate the clinician-plus-model workflow, not the model in isolation, and present calibrated probabilities with the evidence behind them.

Regulation. Software that diagnoses or guides treatment is regulated as a medical device in major markets. That requires a defined intended use, documented validation, quality-system processes and post-market surveillance. Model updates are controlled: a retrained model may need new review unless it falls within a change plan agreed in advance. Continuous deployment and online A/B testing of clinical behaviour are generally not available; evidence comes from retrospective studies, silent-mode prospective runs and clinical trials.

Privacy. Patient data is protected by law (HIPAA in the United States, GDPR in Europe). Consequences: de-identification before research use, access controls and audit logs, contracts with any vendor that processes the data, data that often cannot leave the institution (hence on-premise or federated training), and care with models that may memorise rare records.

Labels and outcomes. Ground truth is expensive expert judgment with real disagreement, outcomes arrive months later, and treatment decisions censor what would have happened.

Equity. Performance must be checked across demographic subgroups, because under-representation in training data translates directly into unequal care.

For LLM products in documentation or patient messaging, the same logic applies: clinician review before anything enters the record, traceability of each statement to its source, and measured rates of omissions and fabrications.

Follow-ups

  • How would you choose the alert threshold with the clinical team?
  • What evidence would you want before letting the model act without review?

coreconceptMLE RE Give me the map of object detection and segmentation: the main families, how they differ, and how they are evaluated.

Detection.

  • Two-stage (Faster R-CNN): a region proposal network suggests boxes, then a second head classifies and refines features pooled from each region. Accurate, especially on small objects; slower.
  • One-stage dense (YOLO family, RetinaNet, anchor-free FCOS and CenterNet): predict class and box at every feature-map location in one pass. Most locations are background, which is the imbalance focal loss addresses, \(-\alpha(1-p_t)^\gamma\log p_t\). Fast; the default for real-time and edge.
  • Set prediction (DETR and successors): a transformer decoder with a fixed set of object queries outputs boxes directly, trained with a one-to-one Hungarian matching loss, so no non-maximum suppression is needed. The original converged slowly and was weak on small objects; deformable attention and improved query designs fixed much of that.

NMS: sort boxes by score and drop those overlapping a kept box above an IoU threshold. It fails in crowds, where true objects overlap.

Metric: average precision is the area under the precision-recall curve for a class at an IoU threshold; COCO mAP averages over classes and IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95, and reports small, medium and large objects separately.

Segmentation.

  • Semantic: a class per pixel (FCN, U-Net, DeepLab); metric mIoU.
  • Instance: a mask per object (Mask R-CNN adds a mask head to a detector); metric mask AP.
  • Panoptic: both, every pixel assigned a class and an instance; metric panoptic quality.
  • Mask classification (Mask2Former style) predicts a set of masks with class labels and unifies the three tasks.
  • Promptable and open-vocabulary models (SAM for class-agnostic masks from points or boxes; text-conditioned detectors) are now the usual starting point for labelling and for long-tail classes.

Different from product ML: labels are expensive dense annotations, errors are spatial rather than a single score, and the deployment constraint is often frames per second on a fixed device.

Follow-ups

  • Why does DETR not need NMS, and what does the matching loss cost?
  • Your detector misses small objects. What do you change first?
coretrade-offMLE RE ViT or CNN for a new vision problem? Explain the trade-off, with numbers where they matter.

Inductive bias versus scale. A CNN builds in locality and translation equivariance, so it learns well from modest data. A ViT splits the image into patches, embeds them as tokens and applies global self-attention from the first layer. It has weaker built-in assumptions, so trained from scratch on small or medium datasets it underperforms a comparable CNN unless heavy augmentation or distillation is used; with large-scale pretraining it matches or exceeds CNNs and keeps improving with data.

Cost. With patch size 16, a \(224\times224\) image is \(14\times14=196\) tokens; at \(1024\times1024\) it is 4,096 tokens, and self-attention is quadratic in that. High-resolution dense prediction therefore uses hierarchical or windowed designs (Swin style), feature pyramids on top of a plain ViT, or token reduction. CNN cost grows linearly with pixels.

What decides it in practice.

  • Pretrained backbones. The strongest general-purpose visual features today come from ViTs pretrained with self-supervision or image-text contrast (DINO-style, masked autoencoding, CLIP-style). If you can fine-tune such a backbone, that usually beats any architecture trained from scratch.
  • Multimodal systems. Patch tokens plug directly into a language model, which is why vision-language models use ViT encoders.
  • Edge and real-time. Convolutions are well optimised on embedded accelerators and quantise predictably; small CNNs or hybrid models often win at a fixed millisecond budget.
  • Small data, no suitable pretraining (unusual sensor, non-natural images): a CNN or a hybrid with a convolutional stem is the safer start.

Modernised CNNs (ConvNeXt) trained with the same recipes close most of the accuracy gap, so much of the reported difference was training recipe and pretraining data, not attention itself. Say which you would pick for the stated data size, resolution and latency target, and why.

Follow-ups

  • Why does a ViT need positional embeddings and what happens when you change resolution?
  • How would you run a ViT backbone on 4K images?
seniorconceptMLE RE You have ten million unlabelled images from your own sensors and five thousand labels. Which self-supervised pretraining would you use, and how does augmentation enter?

First check whether an existing foundation backbone transfers; fine-tuning or linear-probing it on the 5,000 labels is the baseline to beat. In-domain pretraining pays when the sensor or content is far from web images (medical, thermal, industrial, overhead).

Families.

  • Contrastive (SimCLR, MoCo): two augmented views of an image are positives, other images negatives. Learns invariance to the augmentations; needs many negatives; good global features.
  • Self-distillation (DINO family): a student matches an exponential-moving-average teacher across views, with centring and sharpening to avoid collapse. Produces strong frozen features for both image-level and dense tasks.
  • Masked image modelling (MAE): mask a large fraction of patches, around 75%, and reconstruct them. Cheap, because the encoder sees only visible patches, and needs few hand-designed augmentations; features need fine-tuning and are weaker under a linear probe.
  • Image-text contrast (CLIP style): needs paired text; gives semantic, open-vocabulary features, with less spatial detail.

Choice. For dense prediction with fine-tuning, masked modelling or self-distillation; for retrieval or frozen-feature use, self-distillation or contrastive; combine objectives if compute allows. Continue pretraining from a public checkpoint rather than from scratch.

Augmentation is the supervision. In contrastive and distillation methods, the augmentations define what the representation ignores. Colour jitter removes colour information, which is wrong if colour is the label (traffic lights, tissue staining). Random flips are wrong when left-right matters (text, lane direction). Aggressive crops assume the object fills the image, which fails for scenes with many small objects. In supervised fine-tuning, the same logic applies: mixup and CutMix regularise classification; scale jitter, mosaic and copy-paste help detection.

Evaluate the pretrained model on the downstream task with the label budget you have, at several label fractions, and compare against the public-checkpoint baseline.

Follow-ups

  • What does collapse look like and how does each family prevent it?
  • How would you tell whether in-domain pretraining was worth the compute?
corecodingMLE REasked at Apple Implement intersection over union for two sets of boxes. Then implement average precision for one class of an object detector, and say how mean average precision is built from it.

IoU, vectorised over all pairs with broadcasting:

import numpy as np

def iou_matrix(a, b):
    """a: (N, 4), b: (M, 4), boxes as x1, y1, x2, y2. Returns (N, M)."""
    lt = np.maximum(a[:, None, :2], b[None, :, :2])
    rb = np.minimum(a[:, None, 2:], b[None, :, 2:])
    wh = np.clip(rb - lt, 0, None)                  # no overlap gives 0, not a negative area
    inter = wh[..., 0] * wh[..., 1]
    area_a = (a[:, 2] - a[:, 0]) * (a[:, 3] - a[:, 1])
    area_b = (b[:, 2] - b[:, 0]) * (b[:, 3] - b[:, 1])
    union = area_a[:, None] + area_b[None, :] - inter
    return inter / np.maximum(union, 1e-12)

Average precision for one class. Rank every detection of the class across the whole dataset by confidence. Walk down the ranking: a detection is a true positive if its best-overlapping unmatched ground-truth box in the same image has IoU at or above the threshold; otherwise it is a false positive. Each ground-truth box can be matched once, so a second detection of the same object is a false positive.

def average_precision(dets, gts, thr=0.5):
    """dets: list of (image_id, score, box); gts: {image_id: (K, 4) array}."""
    dets = sorted(dets, key=lambda d: -d[1])
    used = {i: np.zeros(len(g), bool) for i, g in gts.items()}
    n_gt = sum(len(g) for g in gts.values())
    tp = np.zeros(len(dets))
    for j, (img, _, box) in enumerate(dets):
        g = gts.get(img)
        if g is None or len(g) == 0:
            continue
        ious = iou_matrix(np.asarray(box, float)[None], g)[0]
        ious[used[img]] = -1
        k = int(ious.argmax())
        if ious[k] >= thr:
            tp[j] = 1; used[img][k] = True
    ctp = np.cumsum(tp)
    recall = ctp / max(n_gt, 1)
    precision = ctp / (np.arange(len(dets)) + 1)
    r = np.concatenate([[0.0], recall, [1.0]])
    p = np.concatenate([[0.0], precision, [0.0]])
    p = np.maximum.accumulate(p[::-1])[::-1]        # precision envelope
    idx = np.nonzero(r[1:] != r[:-1])[0]
    return float(np.sum((r[idx + 1] - r[idx]) * p[idx + 1]))

The envelope replaces each precision by the best precision at any higher recall, which removes the sawtooth; AP is then the area under that step curve.

mAP is the mean of AP over classes. The convention differs by benchmark, so name the one you mean: PASCAL VOC uses IoU 0.5 (and before 2010 an 11-point interpolation); COCO averages AP over IoU thresholds 0.50 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05, samples precision at 101 recall points, caps detections at 100 per image and also reports AP by object size.

Pitfalls an interviewer looks for:

  • Ranking per image instead of across the dataset.
  • Letting one ground-truth box match several detections.
  • A division by zero for a class with no ground truth; decide whether to skip the class or count it as 0, and say which.
  • Pixel conventions: some datasets define width as x2 - x1 + 1.
  • Running the metric after a confidence cut-off. AP needs the low-confidence tail, so evaluate with a very low threshold.

AP summarises ranking quality across all thresholds. It says nothing about calibration or about the operating point you will ship, so a product decision also needs precision and recall at the chosen threshold.

Follow-ups

  • Two detectors have the same mAP at IoU 0.5 and very different COCO mAP. What differs between them?
  • How does non-maximum suppression interact with this metric, and what happens to AP if you remove it?
  • Why can mAP rise while the product metric you care about falls?

coretrade-offMLE RE Compare the classical modular driving stack with end-to-end learned driving. Which would you build and how would you validate it?

Modular. Perception (objects, lanes, free space) feeds prediction (future trajectories of other agents), which feeds planning (a trajectory for the ego vehicle), then control. Interfaces are human-defined: boxes, tracks, lane graphs.

  • For: each module can be developed, tested and debugged separately; intermediate outputs are inspectable; rules and safety constraints can be enforced in the planner.
  • Against: interfaces lose information (a box does not say “pedestrian is looking at their phone”); errors compound across modules; each module is tuned to its own metric, which may not improve driving; hand-written planners scale poorly to rare interactive situations.

End-to-end. One network maps sensor input to a planned trajectory or controls, trained by imitation of human driving, sometimes with reinforcement learning or learned world models.

  • For: optimised jointly for the driving objective, no hand-designed interface bottleneck, improves with data rather than rules.
  • Against: harder to verify and debug; imitation is prone to causal confusion and compounding error away from the demonstrations; needs very large, well-curated data; a failure cannot be assigned to a module.

What is built in practice is usually in between: a jointly trained network that keeps intermediate outputs (detection, occupancy, map, motion) as auxiliary tasks and for inspection, with a learned planner whose output is checked or bounded by explicit safety logic. Which pure form is better is still contested, and public claims are hard to compare.

Validation is the differentiator. Unit metrics per module are not enough for either design. Both need closed-loop evaluation in simulation, scenario-based test suites for rare events, shadow-mode comparison against human drivers on the fleet, and staged road testing. End-to-end systems lean more heavily on closed-loop simulation because there are fewer intermediate outputs to test.

Different from product ML: there is no A/B test with users; errors are safety events, and the validation system is as large an engineering effort as the model.

seniorconceptMLE RE Your planner has a lower displacement error than the baseline on logged data but drives worse in simulation. Explain open-loop versus closed-loop evaluation.

Open loop replays logged sensor data and compares the model’s output with what the human driver did, for example L2 distance between planned and recorded trajectory. The model’s output never affects what it sees next.

Closed loop executes the model’s actions in a simulator or on the road, so the next observation depends on what the model did.

Why they disagree:

  • Compounding error. In open loop every frame starts from a human-driven state. In closed loop a small deviation moves the vehicle to a state slightly off the training distribution, the next output is worse, and errors accumulate.
  • Distance to one human trajectory is a weak proxy. Many trajectories are acceptable; the metric penalises valid alternatives and rewards copying. Mostly straight driving dominates the average, so a model that extrapolates current velocity scores well. It has been shown on public open-loop benchmarks that planners using little more than ego state can be competitive, which is a property of the metric.
  • No interaction. Open loop cannot show whether the model causes other agents to brake or blocks traffic.

What closed-loop simulation needs.

  • Agents: log replay is realistic until the ego deviates, then other agents drive through it; rule-based or learned reactive agents respond but may be unrealistically cooperative or aggressive.
  • Sensors: for camera-based systems, either a graphics simulator (domain gap) or reconstruction of logged scenes with neural rendering, which stays faithful only near the recorded path.
  • Metrics: collisions, off-road, rule violations, progress, comfort (acceleration and jerk), aggregated per scenario type.
  • Validity: the simulator itself must be validated by checking that simulated outcomes predict real outcomes on scenarios where both exist.

Use open-loop metrics for fast iteration and regression checks, and make decisions on closed-loop results. Trajectory prediction for other agents is evaluated open loop with multi-modal metrics: minADE and minFDE over \(K\) predicted modes, and miss rate.

coreconceptMLE RE AI Compare CTC, attention-based encoder-decoder and transducer models for speech recognition. Which would you use for streaming, and how is ASR evaluated?

All three map acoustic frames \(x_{1:T}\) to tokens \(y_{1:U}\) with \(T\gg U\) and unknown alignment; they differ in how alignment is handled.

  • CTC. The encoder emits a distribution over tokens plus a blank at every frame; the probability of \(y\) sums over all frame-level paths that collapse to it, \(P(y\mid x)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}^{-1}(y)}\prod_t p(a_t\mid x)\), computed by dynamic programming. Outputs are conditionally independent given the audio, so there is no internal language model; accuracy improves with an external one. Monotonic, simple, fast, streams easily.
  • Attention encoder-decoder (Whisper is the well-known example). An autoregressive decoder attends over all encoder states. It models token dependencies and gives the best offline accuracy with enough data, but attention over the full utterance is not naturally streaming, and it can hallucinate text on silence or noise and loop on long audio.
  • Transducer (RNN-T). A prediction network encodes the token history, and a joint network combines it with each encoder frame to emit a token or a blank (advance one frame). Monotonic like CTC but conditioned on history like a decoder. It is the standard for streaming and on-device ASR. Training sums over a \(T\times U\) lattice and is memory-heavy.

Streaming requires a causal or limited-lookahead encoder (chunked attention), a monotonic decoder (CTC or transducer), and endpointing. More lookahead gives lower error and higher latency. A common design is a streaming first pass with an attention-based rescoring pass.

Evaluation. Word error rate from the minimum edit distance alignment:

\[\text{WER}=\frac{S+D+I}{N},\]

with substitutions, deletions and insertions over reference words; it can exceed 100%. Pitfalls: text normalisation (numbers, casing, punctuation, contractions) changes WER by several points, so fix one normaliser for all systems; use character error rate for languages without word boundaries; all words count equally, so report entity and number accuracy separately; and slice by accent, noise, far-field and domain, since the average hides the failures. For streaming also report latency to first and final result.

seniorconceptMLE RE Describe a modern text-to-speech pipeline and how TTS quality is evaluated. What has changed with codec language models?

Classical neural pipeline.

  1. Text front end: normalise text (numbers, dates, abbreviations), convert graphemes to phonemes, predict prosodic structure. Errors here cause mispronunciations that no later stage fixes.
  2. Acoustic model: map phonemes to a mel spectrogram. Autoregressive models (Tacotron style) are natural but can skip or repeat words; non-autoregressive models (FastSpeech style) predict durations explicitly and are fast and robust.
  3. Vocoder: convert the mel spectrogram to a waveform, typically a GAN-based model (HiFi-GAN style) that runs much faster than real time.

End-to-end variants merge stages 2 and 3.

Codec language models and flow-based models. A neural audio codec with residual vector quantisation turns audio into a few streams of discrete tokens at tens of frames per second. A language model then generates codec tokens from text, conditioned on a few seconds of reference audio, which gives zero-shot voice cloning and expressive prosody learned from large, loosely transcribed corpora. Non-autoregressive flow-matching models generate mel or latent frames by infilling with similar capabilities and fewer robustness failures. Trade-offs: autoregressive token models can hallucinate, skip or repeat on long or unusual text; quality depends on the codec; and the cloning capability needs consent and misuse controls (watermarking, speaker verification).

Evaluation.

  • Naturalness: mean opinion score or side-by-side preference tests with human listeners; learned MOS predictors for fast iteration, checked against humans.
  • Intelligibility: run an ASR model on the output and compute WER against the input text; catches skipped and repeated words.
  • Speaker similarity: cosine similarity of speaker embeddings between reference and output.
  • Prosody and long-form consistency: mostly human evaluation.
  • Serving: time to first audio and real-time factor (synthesis time divided by audio duration); streaming synthesis must start before the full text is available when the text comes from an LLM.

seniorconceptMLE RE Why do modern perception stacks work in bird’s-eye view, and how do you get image features into BEV?

Why BEV. A top-down metric grid around the ego vehicle is the frame in which planning happens. Objects keep their size regardless of distance, occlusion relations are simpler, multiple cameras and lidar or radar share one coordinate frame, and temporal fusion reduces to warping the previous grid by ego motion.

Lifting camera features. The problem is that a pixel corresponds to a ray, not a point.

  • Depth-based lifting (Lift-Splat style): for each pixel, predict a categorical distribution over depth bins; the outer product of the depth distribution and the pixel’s feature vector places features along the ray; all such points are pooled (“splatted”) into BEV cells. Quality depends on depth accuracy, so later variants supervise depth with lidar.
  • Query-based projection (BEVFormer style): define a grid of learnable BEV queries; each query projects a few 3D reference points at different heights into every camera using the calibration and samples image features there with deformable attention. Temporal self-attention attends to the previous, ego-aligned BEV.
  • Lidar points are voxelised or pillarised into the same grid, so feature-level fusion is concatenation or attention in BEV.

Task heads on the BEV feature map then predict 3D boxes, map elements (lanes, boundaries), motion, or occupancy.

Limits.

  • Camera depth is ambiguous, so localisation error grows with range.
  • Resolution versus range: a \(200\times200\) grid at 0.5 m covers only \(100\times100\) m; longer range or finer cells cost memory quadratically.
  • A 2D grid flattens height, which loses overhanging and irregular obstacles. Occupancy networks predict a 3D voxel grid of occupied space, with semantics and flow, to represent objects that do not fit a box or a known class.
  • Projection depends on calibration; errors corrupt the features silently.

seniorconceptMLE Explain multi-object tracking by detection. Where does the Kalman filter fit, how is association done, and how do you evaluate a tracker?

Tracking turns per-frame detections into identities with a state (position, velocity, extent) over time. The classical loop per frame:

  1. Predict each track’s state with a motion model. For a linear-Gaussian model the Kalman filter gives \(\hat x=Fx\), \(P=FPF^\top+Q\), where \(Q\) is process noise.
  2. Associate detections to predicted tracks. Build a cost matrix from geometric overlap (IoU), Mahalanobis distance under the predicted covariance, and optionally appearance embeddings; gate impossible pairs; solve the assignment with the Hungarian algorithm, or greedily when speed matters.
  3. Update matched tracks: \(K=PH^\top(HPH^\top+R)^{-1}\), \(x\leftarrow\hat x+K(z-H\hat x)\), \(P\leftarrow(I-KH)P\), with \(R\) the measurement noise. The filter weights prediction against measurement by their uncertainties.
  4. Manage tracks: start a track after several consecutive hits, coast it through short occlusions, delete it after several misses.

Non-linear motion (turning) uses an extended or unscented filter or several models in parallel. Learned alternatives carry “track queries” through a transformer so detection and association are trained jointly; they reduce hand-tuning but are harder to debug.

Metrics.

  • MOTA \(=1-\frac{\text{FN}+\text{FP}+\text{ID switches}}{\text{GT}}\): dominated by detection quality.
  • IDF1: F1 over correctly identified detections; measures identity preservation.
  • HOTA: balances detection and association accuracy across localisation thresholds.

Failure modes: identity switches when objects cross or are occluded; ghost tracks from repeated false positives; lag on sudden manoeuvres from an over-smooth motion model; fragmented tracks from intermittent detection. Downstream prediction and planning depend on velocity estimates, so velocity error and track latency should be reported alongside the identity metrics.

coreconceptMLE RE Why is machine learning for trading different from other prediction problems? Cover signal-to-noise, non-stationarity, leakage and backtest overfitting.

Low signal-to-noise. Returns are close to unpredictable. Out-of-sample \(R^2\) for short-horizon return prediction is typically well below 1%, and a correlation of a few percent between forecast and outcome can be valuable when applied across many independent bets. Consequences: strong regularisation and simple models are competitive, small validation differences are noise, and effective sample size is far smaller than the row count because observations overlap and are cross-sectionally correlated.

Non-stationarity and adaptation. Relationships change with market regime, and a profitable signal decays as others discover and trade it. The data-generating process responds to the predictions, unlike most ML settings. Use walk-forward evaluation, check stability across periods, and expect live performance below the backtest.

Leakage.

  • Look-ahead: using data before it was available, such as restated fundamentals, index membership as of today, or the closing price to trade at that close. Requires point-in-time data with availability timestamps.
  • Survivorship: a universe containing only companies that still exist.
  • Overlapping labels: a 20-day forward return at day \(t\) shares 19 days with day \(t+1\), so random K-fold leaks. Use purged cross-validation, which drops training samples whose label windows overlap the test fold, plus an embargo period after it.
  • Normalisation or feature selection fitted on the full sample.

Backtest overfitting. Try enough variants and one will look good by chance: the best Sharpe ratio among \(N\) unskilled strategies grows roughly like \(\sqrt{2\ln N}\) standard errors. Defences: count every trial, deflate the Sharpe ratio for the number of trials, keep a final untouched period, prefer signals with an economic rationale, and check sensitivity to parameters.

Realism. Include transaction costs, bid-ask spread, market impact, borrow costs and capacity; many signals disappear after costs. The objective is risk-adjusted profit after costs, not forecast accuracy.

Latency. In high-frequency trading the budget from market data to order is microseconds or less, which restricts models to very cheap computation, often on specialised hardware; slower strategies run heavier models offline to produce signals.

Base rates. Malicious events are a tiny fraction of an enormous volume. With \(10^9\) events a day and a false-positive rate of \(10^{-4}\), the model raises 100,000 false alerts daily. If only a handful of events are truly malicious, precision is near zero even with perfect recall. An analyst can investigate perhaps tens of alerts a day, so the usable metric is precision at the alert budget and recall of real incidents within it, not accuracy or AUC.

False positives have compounding cost. Each one consumes analyst time, and a noisy detector trains analysts to ignore it, so true alerts get dismissed. Blocking actions (quarantining a file, locking an account) disrupt the business when wrong. Detections are therefore tiered: high-precision automated blocking, medium-confidence alerts with context for triage, and low-confidence signals that are only aggregated or correlated.

Adversaries adapt. The attacker can probe the detector and change behaviour: obfuscate or repack malware, use legitimate administration tools, slow down to stay under thresholds, or poison training data. Unlike natural drift, the shift is aimed at the model’s blind spots. Benign behaviour also drifts with every software update.

Labels are scarce and biased. Ground truth comes from incident investigations and threat intelligence, so it covers what was already caught. “Anomalous” is not “malicious”: pure anomaly detection flags every unusual but legitimate action.

Evaluation that reflects deployment. Split by time, since future attacks differ; split by malware family or campaign, since random splits put near-duplicates on both sides and inflate results; measure time to detect; and run red-team exercises against the full pipeline.

What works. Signals that are costly to evade (behaviour sequences, process and authentication graphs) over content signatures; layering rules, supervised models and anomaly scores; enrichment so that an alert arrives with context and an explanation; short retraining cycles with analyst feedback as labels.

Structure prediction (AlphaFold2 as the reference design). Input is the target sequence plus a multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of evolutionarily related sequences. The signal is co-evolution: positions that mutate together across species tend to be in contact in 3D. The network maintains an MSA representation and a pair representation (one vector per residue pair), updated jointly with attention, including “triangle” updates that encourage geometric consistency among pairs. A structure module then places each residue as a rigid frame using attention that is invariant to global rotation and translation. The output is recycled through the network several times. The model also predicts its own confidence per residue (pLDDT) and per pair, which is used to judge reliability. Training data is experimentally determined structures, on the order of \(10^5\), augmented with self-distillation on predicted structures.

Later systems extend to complexes with ligands and nucleic acids, replacing the structure module with a diffusion model over atom coordinates.

Protein language models (ESM family) are masked language models trained on hundreds of millions of unaligned sequences. Their embeddings encode structure and function, support zero-shot scoring of mutations, and allow single-sequence structure prediction, which is faster and weaker when a deep MSA would have been available. Related generative tools: inverse folding (sequence from backbone) and diffusion models that design backbones.

Equivariance. Physical properties do not depend on the coordinate frame. A function is invariant if \(f(Rx+t)=f(x)\) (energy, binding affinity) and equivariant if \(f(Rx+t)=Rf(x)+t\) (coordinates) or \(Rf(x)\) (forces). Options: build invariance in by using distances and angles; use equivariant layers with geometric tensor features; or use an unconstrained network with random rotations as augmentation. Built-in symmetry is data-efficient and guarantees consistency, at a cost in compute and flexibility; augmentation scales better with data and has been adopted by some recent large models. Which wins depends on data scale.

Limits: a single static structure, weaker results for proteins with few homologues, and confidence that is not a guarantee of functional relevance.

stafftrade-offRE MLE What is different about doing ML for biology or chemistry compared with product ML? Cover data, evaluation splits and the experimental validation loop.

Data is scarce, biased and noisy.

  • Labels come from experiments that cost real money and take days to months, so datasets have hundreds to hundreds of thousands of points, not billions.
  • Selection bias: structures exist for proteins that crystallise; assays were run on compounds chemists chose to make; negative results are rarely published.
  • Measurement noise and batch effects: the same measurement varies across labs, instruments and days, and a model can learn the batch instead of the biology.
  • The assay is a proxy: binding in a tube is not efficacy in an organism.

Random splits overestimate performance. Related sequences and close analogues land on both sides, so the model interpolates. Split by what deployment requires:

  • proteins: cluster by sequence identity and split by cluster;
  • small molecules: split by scaffold;
  • time split, to mimic prospective use;
  • hold out whole targets or assays if the model must generalise to new ones.

Report results under several splits; the gap between random and clustered performance is itself informative.

The validation loop. The model’s purpose is to choose which experiments to run. A design-make-test-learn cycle:

  1. Train on existing data and generate or enumerate candidates.
  2. Select a batch for the lab, balancing predicted value, uncertainty and diversity (batch Bayesian optimisation or active learning), under synthesis and assay constraints.
  3. Measure, add the data, retrain.

Each round takes weeks and returns tens to thousands of labels, so there are few iterations, and uncertainty estimates that hold up under distribution shift matter more than a small gain in held-out accuracy.

What counts as success: prospective hit rate and the quality of the best candidates found per unit of experimental budget, compared with a baseline selection method. Retrospective benchmarks are easy to overfit and often fail to predict prospective results.

Working style: close collaboration with experimentalists, physical priors (symmetry, conservation), and honest baselines, since simple methods on good features are hard to beat on small data.

seniortrade-offMLE AI You are deploying perception or decision-support models on platforms at the edge with limited power and unreliable communications. What changes in how you build and ship them?

Compute is fixed by the platform. Size, weight and power (SWaP) budgets mean an embedded accelerator drawing tens of watts, shared with other software, with thermal limits. The model must fit a hard latency and memory budget on that hardware.

  • Choose architectures for the target runtime, not for leaderboard accuracy.
  • Compress: distillation into a small model, INT8 quantisation (with quantisation-aware training if accuracy drops), pruning, lower input resolution or frame rate, and cascades where a cheap detector triggers an expensive one.
  • Measure on the device: worst-case latency, not the average, and behaviour under thermal throttling.

No dependable link. Communications may be low-bandwidth, intermittent or denied, and satellite positioning may be unavailable.

  • Inference is fully on board; nothing depends on a cloud call.
  • Send compact results (tracks, detections, small crops) rather than raw sensor streams.
  • The system must behave sensibly alone: defined fallbacks, local logging for later upload.

Updating is slow and controlled. Target networks are often isolated from the internet, and software goes through security accreditation.

  • Releases are signed, reproducible artefacts moved through a controlled transfer process; dependencies are vendored and scanned.
  • Update cadence is weeks to months, so robustness to conditions not seen in training is worth more than the last point of accuracy.
  • Telemetry for retraining comes back late and incomplete; plan for it.

Data is scarce and specialised. Sensors include infrared, synthetic-aperture radar and radio-frequency data; labelled examples of the objects of interest are few, and access to data is restricted. Common responses: synthetic data and simulation, transfer from large pretrained models, self-supervised pretraining on unlabelled sensor data, and few-shot adaptation in the field.

Adversarial conditions. Camouflage, decoys, jamming and spoofing are deliberate attempts to defeat sensing, so robustness testing includes them.

Different from product ML: no live experimentation, no quick rollback, a fixed hardware envelope, and a requirement that the system’s limits be documented and communicated to the operator.

staffconceptMLE RE How do you establish that an ML component is reliable enough for a safety- or mission-critical system? Describe the verification and validation approach.

Certification practice for critical software was written for deterministic code traced line by line to requirements. A learned model’s behaviour comes from data, so assurance has to be assembled from several kinds of evidence. Methods for this are still maturing, and a candid answer says so.

1. Define the operating envelope. State the conditions under which the component is claimed to work: sensor types, ranges, weather, lighting, target classes, platform dynamics. Everything outside is out of scope and must be detected at run time.

2. Requirements as measurable criteria. For example probability of detection and false-alarm rate per condition, latency bounds, and behaviour on sensor loss. Trace each requirement to tests.

3. Test across the envelope, not on average. Build a matrix of conditions and report performance per cell with confidence intervals; identify the cells with too little data. Use simulation and synthetic data to fill cells that cannot be collected, and validate the simulator against real data where both exist. Keep test data separate and under configuration control.

4. Robustness and adversarial testing. Sensor degradation, corruption, distribution shift, and deliberate deception. Red-team the component within the whole system.

5. Uncertainty and out-of-distribution handling. The model should report calibrated confidence, and a separate monitor should flag inputs unlike the training distribution. Define what the system does then: abstain, fall back to a simpler method, or hand over to the operator.

6. System-level mitigations. Do not rely on the model alone: redundancy across sensors and algorithms, run-time monitors that check outputs against physical constraints, rule-based safety envelopes around learned behaviour, and defined degraded modes.

7. Human role. Decide which decisions the model informs and which a person makes; present confidence and evidence so the operator can calibrate trust; test the human-machine team, since over-trust and under-trust are both failures.

8. Lifecycle control. Version data, code and models together; a retrained model repeats the test campaign; log inputs and outputs in the field for after-action analysis; monitor for drift.

The deliverable is an assurance case: a structured argument that the residual risk is acceptable, with the evidence for each claim and the limits stated.

coreconceptMLE RE Explain how a latent diffusion model generates an image from text, and derive what classifier-free guidance does.

Latent diffusion runs the diffusion process in the compressed space of an autoencoder instead of in pixels.

  1. Autoencoder. A VAE encoder maps an image to a latent with 8× spatial downsampling, for example \(512\times512\times3\to64\times64\times4\), about 48 times fewer values. The decoder maps latents back to pixels. It is trained separately with reconstruction, perceptual and adversarial losses.
  2. Denoiser. A U-Net or, in newer systems, a transformer over latent patches (DiT) is trained to predict the noise, the velocity or the clean latent from a noised latent \(z_t\), the timestep and the conditioning. Flow-matching variants interpolate linearly between data and noise and regress the velocity.
  3. Conditioning. A text encoder produces token embeddings that enter through cross-attention or by concatenation with the latent tokens.
  4. Sampling. Start from Gaussian noise and integrate the learned reverse process for some tens of steps, then decode. Distilled models reduce this to one to four steps.

Classifier-free guidance. During training the condition is dropped at random (around 10% of the time), so one network learns both the conditional prediction \(\epsilon_\theta(z_t,c)\) and the unconditional \(\epsilon_\theta(z_t,\varnothing)\). At sampling:

\[\hat\epsilon=\epsilon_\theta(z_t,\varnothing)+w\,\big(\epsilon_\theta(z_t,c)-\epsilon_\theta(z_t,\varnothing)\big).\]

Since the noise prediction is proportional to the negative score, this samples from a sharpened distribution \(\propto p(z)\,p(c\mid z)^{w}\): by Bayes’ rule \(\nabla\log p(c\mid z)=\nabla\log p(z\mid c)-\nabla\log p(z)\), so the difference term is the gradient of an implicit classifier, scaled by \(w\). \(w=1\) is plain conditional sampling; typical values are well above 1.

Effects: higher \(w\) improves prompt adherence and perceived quality and reduces diversity; very high values oversaturate and distort. It costs two network evaluations per step unless guidance is distilled into the model. Negative prompts replace the unconditional branch with another condition.

Different from product ML: there is no label to score against, evaluation is distributional or human, and inference cost per sample is high.

seniorconceptRE MLE What makes video generation harder than image generation, what is a “world model” in this context, and how do you evaluate either?

Why video is harder.

  • Token count. A causal 3D autoencoder compresses space (about 8×) and time (about 4×), yet a 5-second 720p clip is still on the order of \(10^5\) latent tokens. Full space-time attention over that is the dominant cost, hence factorised or windowed attention, aggressive compression and few-step samplers.
  • Temporal consistency. Objects must keep identity, shape and position across frames; failures appear as flicker, morphing, objects vanishing behind occluders, and implausible physics.
  • Long duration. Models generate a fixed window. Extending autoregressively (conditioning each chunk on the previous) accumulates error: the model was trained on real context but is fed its own imperfect output. Remedies: noise augmentation of the context during training, per-frame noise levels so that past frames can be cleaner than future ones, and conditioning on longer or retrieved history.

World model. A generative model of future observations conditioned on past observations and on actions, \(p(o_{t+1:t+k}\mid o_{\le t},a_{t:t+k})\). Uses: training policies inside the model (latent-imagination methods such as the Dreamer family), evaluating policies without the real system, generating rare scenarios for driving or robotics, and interactive environments. The distinction from text-to-video is controllability by action and consistency over long interactive rollouts.

Evaluation.

  • Distribution metrics: FID for frames and FVD for clips compare feature statistics of generated and real sets. They need many samples, depend on the feature network, and are reported to track per-frame quality more than temporal coherence.
  • Alignment: text-video similarity scores; decomposed benchmark suites that score subject consistency, motion and so on separately.
  • Human preference: side-by-side comparisons remain the reference for quality.
  • For world models: accuracy of action-conditioned prediction against held-out real rollouts; whether actions have the right effect (controllability); long-horizon stability; and the decisive test, whether policy rankings or failure rates measured inside the model match those measured in the real system.

A visually convincing model can still be useless as a simulator if it ignores actions or hallucinates outcomes that favour the policy.

seniordesignMLE Design ETA prediction for a ride-hailing or delivery product.

1. What is predicted and for whom. Travel time for a route (rider-facing arrival estimate, dispatch cost) and, for delivery, total time including preparation and handoff. Uses differ: the user-facing number should rarely be exceeded, while dispatch wants an unbiased estimate. That affects the loss.

2. Baseline: the routing engine. Map-match the origin and destination, find the route on the road graph, and sum segment travel times from real-time and historical speeds. It is interpretable and handles any origin-destination pair, but has systematic errors: turn and intersection delays, pickup and drop-off time, parking, driver behaviour, stale traffic.

3. Learned correction. Predict the residual between the routing estimate and the observed time, so the model corrects the engine and cannot be wildly wrong when it is unsure.

  • Features: routing estimate and distance; route composition (road classes, number of turns and signals); origin and destination cells (hashed geo cells at several resolutions); time of day, day of week, holidays; real-time traffic and weather; supply and demand conditions; trip type; for delivery, merchant, order size and current kitchen load.
  • Model: gradient-boosted trees or a neural network with embeddings for discretised features; graph neural networks over road segments are used where segment-level traffic is modelled directly. Latency must be milliseconds because dispatch requests many ETAs per decision.
  • Loss: MAE or Huber for a median-like estimate; quantile (pinball) loss for a specific percentile or a range; asymmetric loss when being late costs more than being early.

4. Labels and bias. Completed trips only: cancellations are censored, and the route actually driven may differ from the planned one. Train on features as of request time to avoid leaking traffic that developed later.

5. Evaluation. MAE and relative error by trip-length bucket, city, time of day and conditions; share of trips within a tolerance; tail error (95th percentile); calibration of quantiles; bias. Online, ETA changes affect conversion, cancellations and dispatch efficiency, so experiments need marketplace-aware designs such as switchbacks by region and time.

6. Operations. Retrain frequently; monitor by region for events and road closures; keep a fallback to the routing estimate.

For delivery, model the stages separately (preparation, courier to merchant, wait, courier to customer), since each has its own drivers.

seniorconceptMLE In maps and logistics, where does ML end and optimisation begin? Explain fast routing, dispatch as matching, and the spatial indexes these systems rely on.

The usual division: ML predicts the inputs (travel times, demand, preparation time, acceptance probability) and optimisation decides (routes, assignments) under constraints. Prediction error matters to the extent it changes the decision.

Routing. The road network is a graph with tens of millions of nodes at continental scale. Dijkstra’s algorithm is too slow per query, and A* helps only moderately. Production engines precompute: contraction hierarchies order nodes by importance and add shortcut edges, so a query is a small bidirectional search that only goes “upward”, answering in around a millisecond or less. Live traffic changes edge weights, so variants separate the topology-dependent preprocessing from a fast re-weighting step. Time-dependent travel times make edge weights functions of departure time.

Vehicle routing (many stops, capacities, time windows) is NP-hard. Practice uses construction heuristics plus local search or large-neighbourhood search, or a constraint or integer-programming solver with a time limit, re-optimising as orders arrive.

Dispatch as matching. Collect open requests and available couriers over a short window (seconds), build a cost matrix from predicted pickup times and other terms, and solve a bipartite assignment (Hungarian algorithm, \(O(n^3)\), or min-cost flow), which beats greedily assigning the nearest courier. Refinements: batching several orders on one courier, and accounting for the future, since assigning a courier now removes supply from an area that may need it shortly, handled with forecasted demand or a learned value of having a courier in a place at a time. Pricing and incentives move supply and demand and interact with matching.

Spatial indexing.

  • Geohash: interleaves latitude and longitude bits into a string; prefix means containment; cells vary in size with latitude and neighbours can have unrelated prefixes at boundaries.
  • S2: hierarchical cells on a sphere ordered along a space-filling curve, giving 64-bit ids suited to range scans and region coverings.
  • H3: hexagonal cells whose neighbours are all at about the same distance, convenient for aggregation, smoothing and demand features.
  • R-trees for geometries; map matching of noisy GPS traces to roads with a hidden Markov model.

Experiments in marketplaces suffer interference between units, so region-and-time switchback designs replace user-level A/B tests.
