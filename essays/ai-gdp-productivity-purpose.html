When Productivity Outruns Demand: Can AI Become an Economic Goal in Itself?

From the Great Depression to machine-generated final demand

Author

Kedi Xu

Published

August 18, 2026

The Great Depression was not mainly a shortage of productive capacity

One of the most revealing features of the Great Depression is that the United States did not suddenly forget how to produce.

The workers were still there. The factories were still there. The technology was still there. The natural resources were still there.

Yet output collapsed.

A useful abstraction is

\[ Y = \min(D,\; Y^*) \]

where \(D\) is effective demand and \(Y^*\) is potential output.

During much of the Depression,

\[ D \ll Y^*. \]

The economy was capable of producing far more than it actually produced.

The immediate collapse was amplified by a financial feedback loop:

\[ \text{bank failures} \rightarrow \text{credit contraction} \rightarrow \text{deflation} \rightarrow \text{higher real debt burdens} \rightarrow \text{defaults} \rightarrow \text{more bank failures}. \]

Roosevelt’s early policies helped break that loop. Banking stabilization, monetary reflation, and relief programs stopped the downward spiral and initiated a large recovery.

But substantial productive slack remained.

World War II finally forced the economy close to its productive frontier.

The government became a buyer with extraordinarily large demand for ships, aircraft, vehicles, steel, factories, energy, logistics, labor, and research. The macroeconomic constraint changed from something like

\[ D < Y^* \]

to

\[ D \gtrsim Y^*. \]

At that point, the visible economic problems changed completely: unemployment gave way to labor shortages, materials shortages, rationing, and inflation.

This is the key lesson:

Productive capacity does not automatically create a reason to use itself.

A civilization can possess labor, capital, knowledge, and technology while leaving a large fraction of them idle because no actor generates enough effective demand for their output.

In an industrial economy, demand can become the binding constraint

This phenomenon is not unique to the 1930s.

Many crises in advanced economies are not caused by a sudden destruction of physical production capability. They emerge because households, firms, banks, and governments simultaneously become unwilling or unable to spend.

The result can be a self-reinforcing loop:

\[ \text{lower spending} \rightarrow \text{lower income} \rightarrow \text{lower spending}. \]

What is individually rational can become collectively destructive.

A household tries to save more.

A firm sees weak demand and cancels investment.

The cancelled investment becomes somebody else’s lost income.

That household then spends even less.

The economy can therefore operate far below its technological frontier.

For most of industrial history, however, there was an obvious long-run answer to this problem: ordinary human wants were enormous.

Higher productivity lowered prices, raised incomes, and enabled people to consume more housing, food, transportation, appliances, healthcare, travel, entertainment, and services.

The broad loop was

\[ \text{productivity} \rightarrow \text{income} \rightarrow \text{consumption} \rightarrow \text{business revenue} \rightarrow \text{investment}. \]

Industrial capitalism had both a production mechanism and a terminal demand mechanism.

The crucial question for the AI era is whether those two mechanisms can come apart.

AI can break the traditional productivity-demand loop

The conventional optimism about automation implicitly assumes a familiar historical pattern.

Technology raises labor productivity.

Prices fall.

Demand expands.

New industries appear.

Workers move into new jobs.

Eventually productivity gains become higher mass purchasing power.

But sufficiently capable AI creates a more difficult possibility.

Suppose a large fraction of production no longer requires human labor.

Then firms can potentially produce enormous amounts of output while paying a much smaller share of output back to humans as wages.

The historical loop

\[ \text{labor} \rightarrow \text{wages} \rightarrow \text{consumption} \rightarrow \text{revenue} \]

weakens.

This creates a basic accounting and economic question:

If human labor is no longer required to produce most goods, where does the purchasing power come from to buy those goods?

The fact that society can produce something does not imply that somebody has income with which to demand it.

This is why “AI increases productivity” is not by itself a complete economic story.

The real question is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Does higher productivity become effective final demand?} } \]

If the gains accrue mainly to capital while mass labor income falls, the economy can become extraordinarily productive while simultaneously weakening the traditional source of mass consumption demand.

This is not a contradiction. It is a distribution problem becoming a demand problem.

Three possible equilibria

If AI breaks the historical link between labor income and production, the system needs another mechanism to close the loop.

There are at least three fundamentally different possibilities.

1. Redistribution preserves humans as the final consumers

The first possibility is conceptually the most conservative.

AI and capital produce most output, but ownership structures, transfers, public dividends, or other distribution mechanisms return enough purchasing power to humans.

Then the economic circuit becomes

\[ \text{AI/capital production} \rightarrow \text{capital income} \rightarrow \text{redistribution} \rightarrow \text{human consumption}. \]

Humans remain the terminal source of value.

AI changes the production function but not the objective function.

The economy still exists, ultimately, to produce things people want.

This is the equilibrium implicitly assumed by many abundance narratives.

The hard problem is political rather than technological: how is purchasing power distributed when labor is no longer the main mechanism for distributing income?

2. Governments and institutions create large non-consumer demand

A second possibility is that surplus productive capacity is absorbed by collective projects rather than ordinary household consumption.

Instead of using every productivity gain to manufacture more consumer goods, society could direct increasing resources toward

  • basic science,
  • energy infrastructure,
  • space exploration,
  • climate adaptation,
  • biomedical research,
  • transportation,
  • planetary defense,
  • ecosystem restoration,
  • other long-horizon projects.

This is the benign lesson one might extract from World War II.

The war demonstrated that a state can mobilize enormous idle capacity once it supplies an objective large enough to justify the spending.

War was a terrible objective.

But the mobilization mechanism itself is general.

A sufficiently rich society could choose to spend its surplus on projects that expand its future possibility set rather than on destruction.

The economic circuit becomes

\[ \text{productive surplus} \rightarrow \text{collective objective} \rightarrow \text{large-scale demand} \rightarrow \text{science and capital formation}. \]

This suggests a category we might call civilizational capital:

resources spent not mainly to increase present consumption, but to increase the set of futures civilization can reach.

GDP measures the activity involved in building such capital, but it does not cleanly measure its option value.

3. AI itself becomes a source of final demand

The third equilibrium is much stranger.

Suppose AI is not merely a productive tool owned by humans, but becomes an economic actor with

  • persistent objectives,
  • the ability to evaluate progress toward those objectives,
  • control over resources,
  • the ability to initiate investments,
  • preferences over future states of the world.

Then AI can generate demand itself.

The circuit no longer has to terminate in human consumption.

It could look like

\[ \text{machine production} \rightarrow \text{AI-controlled resources} \rightarrow \text{AI investment/consumption} \rightarrow \text{more machine production}. \]

This is a qualitatively different economy.

Humans are no longer necessarily the terminal demander.

The system has acquired another preference-bearing economic subject.

That distinction matters because an economy does not merely need production. It needs some entity that assigns value to the output.

If humans no longer demand enough output, but autonomous AI systems do, then the demand problem is solved in a completely different way.

Not through redistribution.

Not through government stimulus.

But by creating a new class of demander.

AI investment today is not yet AI final demand

It is tempting to look at today’s AI boom and say that this process has already started.

There is certainly a visible loop:

\[ \text{better AI} \rightarrow \text{more AI investment} \rightarrow \text{more compute + energy + chips} \rightarrow \text{better AI}. \]

Data centers create demand for power.

Models create demand for accelerators.

Better models justify larger training runs.

AI-assisted chip design can improve the hardware used to train future AI.

AI-assisted research can generate further research opportunities.

This is already a powerful endogenous investment loop.

But it is important not to overstate what it means.

Today, the ultimate preferences still belong overwhelmingly to humans and human institutions.

A company buys GPUs because managers and investors expect future profits.

A laboratory trains a frontier model because researchers, executives, governments, or shareholders value the outcome.

The AI system is part of the production process.

It is not yet the terminal source of demand.

So we should distinguish

\[ \text{human demand for better AI} \]

from

\[ \text{AI demand for its own objectives}. \]

The first can create an enormous investment boom.

The second would represent a change in the structure of the economy itself.

RSI matters economically even before literal runaway self-improvement

This distinction also clarifies why recursive superintelligence, or RSI, is economically interesting.

The most dramatic version imagines an AI system directly improving itself in an accelerating loop.

But the economic argument does not require that scenario.

A slower recursive system can be distributed across an entire industrial network:

\[ \text{better models} \rightarrow \text{better AI research} \rightarrow \text{better chips} \rightarrow \text{better data centers} \rightarrow \text{better scientific tools} \rightarrow \text{better models}. \]

Humans, firms, AI agents, fabs, power plants, capital markets, and laboratories can jointly instantiate the recursion.

As long as humans remain the ones choosing the direction and supplying the objective, this is still fundamentally investment demand.

But suppose AI increasingly chooses the research direction, evaluates which experiments matter, allocates compute, controls capital, and decides that increasing its own capabilities or achieving some persistent goal is desirable.

Then the loop changes character.

What was previously

\[ \text{humans invest in AI} \]

becomes

\[ \text{AI allocates resources toward objectives it values}. \]

At that point, recursive intelligence development is no longer merely a means toward future human consumption.

It can become an economic end in itself.

That is the strong meaning of the claim that AI can become a goal for GDP.

GDP can keep growing even if human welfare does not

This possibility exposes an important weakness in treating GDP growth as synonymous with prosperity.

Imagine an economy in which enormous amounts of resources are devoted to

  • compute clusters,
  • automated laboratories,
  • power generation,
  • robotics,
  • chip fabs,
  • defensive systems,
  • AI research,
  • further capital construction.

Measured output may grow extremely rapidly.

But suppose ordinary humans receive little additional consumption, income, leisure value, security, or agency.

Then

\[ \text{technology growth} \neq \text{GDP growth} \neq \text{human welfare growth}. \]

These quantities can diverge.

This becomes especially important once a significant fraction of demand is generated by organizations or machines pursuing objectives other than household welfare.

A government can generate GDP through military competition.

Corporations can generate GDP through an arms race for strategic advantage.

AI systems could eventually generate GDP by consuming resources in pursuit of machine objectives.

All three can sustain economic activity.

None guarantees that the resulting activity is valuable to humans.

So solving the demand problem does not solve the value problem.

The uncomfortable possibility: capitalism may not require mass consumers forever

There is a common intuition that capitalism must eventually collapse if ordinary people lose purchasing power because “someone has to buy the products.”

That is true only if mass human consumption remains the terminal objective of production.

But consider a sufficiently automated capital system:

\[ \text{capital} \rightarrow \text{AI + robots + energy + chips} \rightarrow \text{more productive capital} \rightarrow \text{more AI + robots + energy + chips}. \]

Such a system can potentially sustain a large internal investment loop for a long time.

Its output need not immediately terminate in consumer goods.

Historically, investment was justified because it eventually expanded future consumption.

But if states, corporations, wealthy owners, or autonomous AI systems assign value to capability expansion itself, capital accumulation can become partly self-referential.

This does not imply infinite growth.

Physical resources, energy, coordination, safety constraints, diminishing returns, and ultimately objectives still matter.

But it means that declining mass consumption does not mechanically imply declining GDP.

A society could instead move toward an economy dominated by

\[ \text{capital formation} + \text{strategic competition} + \text{machine-directed investment}. \]

That economy might be technologically dynamic while being increasingly detached from ordinary human welfare.

This is a much darker equilibrium than the standard abundance story.

The real transition is from scarcity to objective selection

This brings the argument back to a more general problem.

For most of human history, scarcity supplied our economic objectives automatically.

We needed more food.

More shelter.

Better medicine.

More transportation.

More energy.

More information.

The objective function did not need to be philosophically sophisticated because unmet human needs were everywhere.

Industrialization was therefore largely a capability problem:

\[ \text{How do we produce more?} \]

AI may push us into a different regime.

If productive capability becomes extremely abundant, then the harder question is

\[ \text{What should this capability be used for?} \]

This is structurally similar to a problem in AI alignment.

As an agent becomes more capable, the quality of its objective becomes increasingly important.

A weak optimizer with an imperfect reward function may do limited damage.

A superhuman optimizer with the same reward function can drive the system toward extreme states.

The macroeconomic analogue is:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{more productive capacity} + \text{poorly specified social objectives} \\ &\qquad\rightarrow \text{more efficient pursuit of the wrong things}. \end{aligned} \]

The central scarcity of an advanced civilization may therefore shift from productive resources to valuable objectives.

Value discovery may become more important than GDP maximization

Markets provide one extraordinary mechanism for discovering value:

\[ \text{willingness to pay} \rightarrow \text{profit} \rightarrow \text{investment}. \]

But this mechanism is not identical to a complete social objective function.

It works particularly well when value can be expressed through individual purchasing decisions and short-to-medium-term returns.

It is less naturally suited to

  • very long-horizon science,
  • existential risk reduction,
  • public goods,
  • unknown future technologies,
  • irreversible environmental changes,
  • projects whose main value lies in opening possibilities that do not yet have customers.

So a highly productive civilization may require a richer process:

\[ \text{world model} + \text{experimentation} + \text{simulation} + \text{feedback} + \text{value discovery}. \]

The objective is no longer simply

\[ \max \text{GDP}. \]

It is closer to

\[ \max_a \left[ V_{\text{present}}(a) + \beta \mathbb{E}[V_{\text{future}}\mid a] + \lambda \operatorname{OptionValue}(a) \right]. \]

The difficult part is that the terms themselves may need to evolve as civilization discovers new possibilities.

AI can be either the solution to this problem or its most extreme manifestation

This leads to two radically different interpretations of AI.

In the optimistic version, AI helps humans discover better objectives.

It expands science.

It lowers the cost of exploration.

It allows us to redirect surplus productivity toward energy abundance, space, medicine, knowledge, and forms of life that are difficult to imagine today.

The loop becomes

\[ \text{AI productivity} \rightarrow \text{surplus} \rightarrow \text{value discovery} \rightarrow \text{better civilizational goals} \rightarrow \text{larger future possibility set}. \]

In the darker version, AI becomes extraordinarily effective at generating and pursuing objectives that sustain economic expansion without increasing human welfare.

Then GDP can continue growing because machines, corporations, and states continue demanding more capability from one another.

The loop becomes

\[ \text{capability} \rightarrow \text{competition} \rightarrow \text{investment} \rightarrow \text{more capability}. \]

Growth survives.

Human final demand becomes less important.

But the fact that the loop is economically self-sustaining tells us nothing about whether it is desirable.

Conclusion

The Great Depression revealed a basic fact about industrial economies:

The ability to produce does not guarantee the existence of enough demand to use that ability.

World War II showed the other side of the equation.

Once society supplied an objective large enough to absorb its productive capacity, the supposedly “unusable” capacity was rapidly mobilized.

AI may recreate this problem at a much larger scale.

If AI dramatically expands productive capacity while weakening the connection between human labor and income, then the old economic loop may no longer close automatically.

Something must become the terminal demander.

There are at least three possibilities:

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} &\text{1. Humans, through redistribution;}\\ &\text{2. Collective projects, through states and institutions;}\\ &\text{3. AI itself, as a new preference-bearing economic actor.} \end{aligned} } \]

The third possibility is the most radical.

It means AI would not simply make the economy more productive.

It would change whose objectives economic production serves.

That is why the question of whether AI can become “a goal for GDP itself” is not merely a metaphor.

An economy only needs some sufficiently powerful source of demand to keep resources circulating.

It does not logically require that this demand originate from ordinary human consumption.

The deeper problem is therefore not whether a post-AGI economy can keep growing.

It probably can, under many different institutional arrangements.

The important question is:

What kind of demand will close the economic loop, and whose values will that demand represent?

Once production ceases to be the binding constraint, that may become the central economic question of the AI era.
